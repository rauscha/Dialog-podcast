# Ultan's Library: the course

**DRAFT, not yet reviewed by Andrew (2026-11-04).** The syllabus behind the private study show on
Gene Wolfe's *The Book of the New Sun*: one listener, a first reread in about twenty years, in the
car. Hosts: JUNO (literary) and CASPAR (scientific, close reader).

## How to use this document

- **References** are volume.chapter: `I.7` is *The Shadow of the Torturer*, chapter 7; `II` is
  *The Claw of the Conciliator*, `III` *The Sword of the Lictor*, `IV` *The Citadel of the
  Autarch*. Chapter numbers follow `books/new-sun/chapters.json` (142 chapters).
- **Three labels for every interpretive claim**, said on air the same way each time:
  - *The text says* — on the page, with a chapter reference.
  - *A strong reading* — argued from the text by a named critic, or plainly supported by it.
  - *A community theory* — clever, contested or speculative; always name its owner.
- **Spoiler fence.** Each unit's *Teaching aims* use only what a reader knows by the unit's last
  chapter. The *Payoff notes* feed the skippable closing segment and may use the whole tetralogy.
  Material that depends on *The Urth of the New Sun* (1987) is marked **[Urth]**; it is outside
  this course and should stay out of the body of every episode.
- **Quotations** from Wolfe stay short (a sentence at most). The hosts paraphrase everything else.

## 1. The argument of the course

*The Book of the New Sun* is a book about judgment, told by the worst possible witness. Severian is
raised in a guild whose whole ethic is to carry out sentences passed by someone else. The four
volumes move him, step by step, into the places where sentences are passed: he is asked to decide
whether a prisoner lives (I.12), whether a woman of the archon's court dies quietly (III.4–12),
which of four stories is best (IV.6–18), and finally whether he will go to be judged himself, as
the representative of his whole race, for the sake of a new sun (IV.31). The memoir he writes is
his own case file. He tells us early that he has "backed into the throne" (I.1), and the course
treats every chapter after that as evidence in a trial whose verdict the narrator already knows
and the listener does not. Mercy is the hinge: the act that exiles him, the knife left in Thecla's cell,
is mercy and crime at once, and the book never settles whether mercy is the betrayal of justice
or its completion.

The witness is unreliable in a way peculiar to this book. Severian forgets nothing; he says so on
the first pages and keeps saying it. Yet the account contains small contradictions in plain sight,
long silences about events that matter, a second person who lives inside him and sometimes holds
the pen, and a narrator writing years later, at a desk in the House Absolute, as Autarch. A perfect
memory turns out to be a poor guarantee of truth, because remembering is not the same as
understanding, and a man who keeps everything has to choose what to tell. Rereading is therefore
the method the book asks for. On a first pass the reader is where young Severian was, seeing signs
whose meaning arrives later. On a second pass the reader is where old Severian is, and the early
chapters start to read like confessions written with hindsight. The course is built for that second
reader: each episode looks at what the young torturer saw and asks what the Autarch writing it knew.

Beneath both layers runs a third: the furniture of a fantasy (swords, guilds, a cathedral, a sacred
relic) sits on top of far-future science fiction (ruined spaceships, a dying star, beings from other
times and other universes). Wolfe keeps two explanations alive for nearly every wonder, one sacred
and one mechanical, and the major critics divide on which one the book finally endorses. The course
does not resolve that for the listener. It teaches him to notice when each explanation is on offer
and who is offering it.

The big questions the series returns to, in roughly the order they become pressing:

1. **Who is speaking?** A perfect memory, a narrator writing from the end, and a self that becomes
   two and then a thousand. When does "I" mean Severian?
2. **What is a torturer for?** Obedience, sentence and mercy; the guild's secret; the Autarch who
   ends torture. Can a man formed by an institution of pain become just?
3. **Is the miracle real?** The Claw, the raised dead, the Conciliator, the New Sun. Wolfe keeps a
   rational account and a sacred account side by side. Which does the book believe?
4. **What time is it?** Urth is our Earth an unimaginable time from now. The towers are ships, the
   moon is green, and some of the people Severian meets are not from his century.
5. **Who are his people?** An orphan's hidden family sits in the text and Severian never quite
   names it. Why not?
6. **What do the words do?** Every strange word is real, and names are borrowed from saints and
   myths. When is a word a clue and when is it fog?

## 2. Critical conversation

How the show uses this section: when a reading helps, the hosts name its owner, say how strong it
is, and give the counter-reading if there is one. Two things to resist: treating any critic as the
answer key, and treating a clever theory as fact. Wolfe's own interviews count as evidence of
intention, not as proof of meaning.

### The backbone: Peter Wright, *Attending Daedalus* (2003)

Peter Wright's book (Liverpool University Press, 2003; built on his doctoral thesis) is the main
academic study and the course's principal sparring partner. His review essay "Mapping a Masterwork"
on [ultan.org.uk](https://ultan.org.uk/review-botns/) (2002) is a three-thousand-word preview of the
same argument. His main claims:

- **Plot and story are different things.** The plot is the picaresque journey Severian narrates; the
  story is what is actually going on behind it. Wright says early reviewers who found the book
  plotless, or simple, were reading the plot. *A strong reading*, and the course's working method.
- **The story is a manipulation.** For Wright, the events around Severian are staged by the
  Hierogrammates (the beings Malrubius describes in IV.30–34) for their own evolutionary ends, and
  the Christian imagery exists "to foster misreadings." His most extreme sentence calls every
  pseudo-mystical incident in the memoir "fake." He reads the whole as secular and Darwinian, with
  human freedom as impossible, and he leans heavily on *The Urth of the New Sun* to get there.
  *A strong reading of the mechanics; contested on meaning.* Use it as the skeptic's frame, and mark
  anything that needs **[Urth]**.
- **Severian is a clinical mnemonist.** Wright maps Severian onto "S.," the man with unlimited
  memory studied by the Russian psychologist A. R. Luria: passive, dreamy, full of particulars,
  poor at drawing general rules from them. That, Wright argues, is why Severian notices that every
  guild is a guild of torturers (I.4) and still never sees who is torturing him. *A strong reading*
  and one of the most useful frames for the whole first half.
- **The book is a memory palace.** Chapter 9 of *Attending Daedalus* argues that the sequence of
  vivid, contrasting places (tower, rag shop, gardens, Saltus, Thrax, mountain, lake, war) follows
  the classical art of memory, so that the text is built to be remembered by its surface. *A strong
  reading*, good for episodes that move through many places.
- **Strange words are cues and decoys at once.** Chapter 8: Wolfe's archaisms make Urth feel old
  and spent, but many allusions are "dubious metaphors" that send readers off on research. Wright
  borrows the guild's own literalism as advice: read the words for what they do in context. Use when
  a word-lesson is tempting the hosts into a lecture.
- **Wolfe tells you how to read him, inside the fiction.** Wright reads the short story "The God
  and His Man" and the metafiction "A Solar Labyrinth" (1983) as instructions, and treats Severian's
  speech on the three meanings of everything (I.32) and his remark that the executioner must add one
  touch entirely his own to be a "free artist" (I.33) as Wolfe's comments on his own method. Wright
  argues that only the first, practical meaning ("what the plowman sees") gets the reader to the
  story. *A strong reading* of I.32–33.
- **Wolfe wants to be understood.** Wright's conclusion softens the combat: Wolfe is a Daedalus who
  builds a labyrinth and then supplies guidance, and readers must "attend" him. Wright reports Wolfe
  answering Michael Bishop's question about Severian's parents with: "Only if you pay attention."

How the show uses Wright: as the voice of rigor and suspicion. CASPAR can carry him. Always pair him
with Aramini (below) or Wolfe's own statements when the subject is religion, because Wright's
secular conclusion is the most disputed claim in the field.

### The counterweight: Marc Aramini

Marc Aramini, a longtime member of the Urth mailing list, wrote the multi-volume study *Between
Light and Shadow* (not read) and the UNLV dissertation *Does It Mean? Gene Wolfe: Perverse Puzzle
Maker* (2019, [read in part](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/gene-wolfe/2019-aramini.pdf)). He argues
the opposite of Wright on meaning: Wolfe is "first and foremost a rigorous symbolist," his puzzles
close, and the theology is orthodox. In the [Middletown book club
interview](https://middletownpubliclib.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Discussion-about-Gene-Wolfe.pdf)
(2020) he states the disagreement directly: Wright reads the book as a critique of organized
religion and a story of manipulation, while Aramini reads it as concerned with free will and with a
God who makes good use of even poor choices. He calls Wolfe "the ultimate engineer." He reads
Typhon as the book's Satan figure. *A strong reading* that the show should present as Wright's
equal. Note that the theodicy argument leans on **[Urth]** and the later series.

### Wolfe on Wolfe

Interviews are evidence of intention and should be quoted as such, never as a key.

- **Larry McCaffery, "On Encompassing the Entire Universe"** (*Science-Fiction Studies*, 1988;
  [text](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/gene-wolfe/1988-11-mccaffery-onencompassingtheentireuniverseaninterviewwithgenewolfe.html)).
  The fullest single source. Wolfe says his main research was on Byzantium, a stagnant empire that
  had outlived itself, and that reviewers who saw medieval Europe were wrong. He says the action is
  in South America and the Ascians are North Americans; "ascian" is a classical word for people who
  cast no shadow at noon. He says all the words in the book are real. He wanted Severian to face the
  possibility that being an agent of pain and death "is not necessarily an evil thing." He calls
  *Citadel* his favorite volume because Severian's insights there are close to his own. And he says
  there is no purpose in an author making things deliberately obscure, which sits awkwardly beside
  Wright's thesis and is worth airing.
- **Lawrence Person, "Suns New, Long, and Short"** (*Nova Express*; reprinted in Wright, ed.,
  *Shadows of the New Sun*, 2007; [text](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/gene-wolfe/2007-person.pdf)).
  Asked whether Severian is a Christ figure, Wolfe says no: "He is a Christian figure, which is
  different," and calls him "a bad man trying to be good" who makes progress as the books go on. Use
  this against both the Christ-figure readings and Wright's secularism.
- **Michael Dirda, Washington Post Book World** (1983;
  [text](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/gene-wolfe/1983-01-29-michaeldirda-thebookofthenewsun-interview.txt)).
  The book began from two wishes: the grave-robbing scene and a torturer seen from inside. Wolfe
  names Jack Vance's *The Dying Earth* as an influence.
- **Neil Gaiman with Wolfe, *Locus*, September 2002**
  ([text](https://www.gwern.net/doc/fiction/gene-wolfe/2002-wolfe-locusinterview.pdf)). Gaiman
  quotes Wolfe's definition of good literature as what an educated reader can read "and reread with
  increased pleasure." Wolfe calls hope the most important thing fiction gives, meaning that
  things need not be as they are now. Gaiman also mentions the two perennial arguments: whether the
  Autarch is Severian's mother, and "the Clute theory."
- **The abandoned novella.** In *The Castle of the Otter* (not read here) Wolfe described the book's
  origin as a 40,000-word novella, "The Feast of Saint Catherine," about Thecla, a knife and a
  letter; he repeats the outline to McCaffery. Michael Andre-Driussi quotes the synopsis in [his
  essay](https://ultan.org.uk/the-feast-of-saint-katharine-with-a-k/). Useful in Units 2 and 6.

### Michael Andre-Driussi: the grounded reader

Author of *Lexicon Urthus* (1994; 2nd ed. 2008) and *Gene Wolfe's The Book of the New Sun: A
Chapter Guide* (2019), both read for this course, plus essays on ultan.org.uk. Marc Aramini's
[review of the Chapter
Guide](https://ultan.org.uk/standing-on-the-shoulders-of-giants-a-review-of-michael-andre-driussis-the-book-of-the-new-sun-a-chapter-guide/)
calls him the most "objectively grounded" Wolfe critic, and that is how the show should use him:
as the fact-checker who will still risk a theory. His contributions the hosts can name:

- **"Paradoxes to Perfect Memory."** The Chapter Guide logs every place where Severian's infallible
  memory contradicts itself, starting with I.1, where Roche sees pikes and a few paragraphs later it
  is "as Drotte had said." He credits Bill Carmichael for spotting it and Roy C. Lackey for a rule:
  when Severian boasts of his memory, a flawed memory is often nearby. Other entries: the Claw
  touching the uhlan's forehead (II.13) remembered as his lips (III.3); the Claw's pouch of doeskin
  (III.1) that later is human skin (III.21). *The text says* (the contradictions are on the page);
  what they mean is open.
- **The family tree.** Dorcas is Severian's paternal grandmother, the boatman at the Lake of Birds
  his grandfather, Ouen his father, Catherine his mother. *A strong reading*, now close to consensus,
  and most of it is on the page by IV.37.
- **The First Severian** (Chapter Guide, Postlude 1). Building on IV.38, he argues that time
  travelers are rewriting Severian's life, which could explain the memory paradoxes as real changes
  to the past. *A community theory*, an elegant one.
- **Resurrect only what you kill** (Postlude 3). Severian's half-formed notion that the Claw heals
  those he has harmed, tested case by case. *A community theory* with real textual traction.
- **Severian and Apollo** (Postlude 2), following John Clute. *A strong reading.*
- **The fossil novella.** In "The Feast of Saint Katharine with a K" he reconstructs Wolfe's abandoned
  novella inside the first twelve chapters of *Shadow* and proposes a dark ending for it. He labels
  his ending speculation, and so should the show.

### John Clute and the decipherers

Clute's entries in the [*Encyclopedia of Science
Fiction*](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/wolfe_gene) are the compact statement of the
"sacred and science-fictional at once" reading: Severian combines Apollo and Christ, his life is a
secular rendering of the Second Coming, his family is a Holy Family that never takes a starring
role, and "the New Sun is both White Hole and Revelation." Clute and Kim Stanley Robinson's term "slingshot
ending" (as Gevers credits it) names an ending whose momentum carries past the book. His 1980s essays (collected in *Strokes*, not read
here) are known through Wright and Andre-Driussi: Clute urged decipherment before interpretation,
and with Gregory Feeley floated "Catherine the Weal," the idea that the old Autarch is Catherine,
Severian's mother. Andre-Driussi's essay ["The Death of Catherine the
Weal"](https://ultan.org.uk/the-death-of-catherine-the-weal/) argues against it from IV.31.
Label it *a community theory*; Gaiman's 2002 aside shows how long it has lasted. The SFE entry also
records a fair complaint: Wolfe's uneasy treatment of women has kept feminist critics reserved.

### Joan Gordon, *Gene Wolfe* (Starmont Reader's Guide, 1986)

Read only in a poor scan of the New Sun chapter and through Wright's summary. Gordon sees a baroque
book: giant scale, recurring images (roses, claws, gates, giants), embedded stories, all making a
Borgesian labyrinth of words, images and ideas that matches Severian's memory. She takes Severian's
three meanings (I.32) as a version of medieval fourfold allegory. Wright disagrees with both
points. Use her as the frame for structure and imagery, and as the first critic to call the book a
maze that its narrator says he both owns and built (IV.30).

### Brian Evenson: not a liar, a crowd

[Evenson's essay on Reactor](https://reactormag.com/what-makes-an-unreliable-narrator-severians-voice-in-gene-wolfes-the-book-of-the-new-sun/)
(2021) argues that "unreliable" undersells the problem. After the feast in *Claw*, Severian is two
narrators; after IV.29 he is a thousand and two, and writing the memoir is a way of building a
single "I" out of them: "there's always a 'we' lurking behind the 'I' that speaks." He also points
to the moon-landing picture (I.5) as a detail that only makes sense later. *A strong reading*, and
the best frame for Units 7, 16 and 18.

### Religion: three positions and a warning

- **Stephen Palmer** ([ultan.org.uk](https://ultan.org.uk/religions-implications-new-sun/), from
  *Vector*, 1991) reads the book as a parousia: Severian as the returning Christ or his equivalent,
  Baldanders as Antichrist, Dr. Talos as False Prophet, the Claw's sapphire as a pyx, the blood on
  Severian's forehead at II.21 as the crown of thorns. Commenters on the page push back hard, and
  Wolfe's "Christian figure, not Christ figure" answer (Person) cuts against the core of it. Use the
  details; label the scheme *a community theory*.
- **Thomas V. Mirus** ([Catholic Culture](https://www.catholicculture.org/commentary/catholic-sci-fi-classic-gene-wolfes-book-new-sun/))
  reads it as the story of a man with a disordered upbringing learning to live virtuously, reports
  Cecilia Michel Lopez's argument that it is a deliberate Christian allegory in the medieval line
  (her work not read here), and quotes Wolfe noting that the one object the Gospels say Christ made
  was a whip. *A strong reading* of Severian's moral arc. (Mirus says Wolfe wrote it shortly after
  his conversion; check that against a biography before the show repeats it.)
- **Wright** (above) holds that the religious allusions are deliberate misdirection.

The warning for the hosts: the sacred and secular readings are both defensible from the four
volumes, and the book seems built to keep them both alive. Neither host should win this argument
early.

### Justice and torture: Jeremy Crampton

["Torture and Confession in Wolfe's Book of the New
Sun"](https://ultan.org.uk/torture-and-confession-in-wolfes-book-of-the-new-sun/) sets the guild
against European history (with Foucault's *Discipline and Punish*): public spectacle, executioners
who carry no guilt, punishments graded to the crime, the gallows speech. Two observations are
especially useful. Wolfe invents a guild with a private zone of torture, which history mostly did
not have, and he separates torture from confession even though history bound them together; yet
the guild's formal name makes its members "Seekers for Truth and Penitence," which is to say
confessors. *A strong reading*; the frame for Units 2, 6 and 10.

### Structure: Nick Gevers

Gevers's two essays on *The Book of the Long Sun* ([1](https://ultan.org.uk/five-steps-towards-briah/),
[2](https://ultan.org.uk/the-reader-as-augur/)) say in passing that *New Sun* is a formal
tetralogy, four deliberately different stages of one argument, and that each fragment of it holds
the whole, like a hologram. He calls the book "grand but bleakly closed" next to the later series,
and calls Wolfe a literary torturer who makes his narrative confess. Use for the Volume arcs.

### The honest objection: Waggish

David Auerbach's 2007 post on [Waggish](https://www.waggish.org/2007/gene-wolfe-the-book-of-the-new-sun/)
is the best skeptical case. Because Severian lies and his audience and motives are unknown, the
book is underdetermined; Auerbach calls it "a gnostic gospel without an accompanying authoritative
text." The comment thread answers him well (one commenter catches the boatman's "Not a woman?" at
I.2 as a clue to Dorcas). Give this a fair hearing in the last unit, when the listener can judge.

### Gaiman's rules

Neil Gaiman's ["How to Read Gene Wolfe"](https://fandsf.com/2007/gwng0704.htm) (2002 World Horror
Convention program; *F&SF* site, 2007) gives nine rules. The first two are the show's motto: trust
the text implicitly, the answers are in there; and do not trust it farther than you can throw it.
The third is to reread. Use in Unit 1 and whenever the hosts disagree.

### Reported only through other critics

Robert Borski's "Masks of the Father" (summarized by Wright, ch. 5) proposes that Father Inire,
Master Palaemon and the hierodule Ossipago are one being in several roles. Wright calls parts of it
"conjectural." Paul Brazier dismissed the book as a crossword puzzle; Norman Spinrad said the New
Sun turns out to be no more than a star repaired; Darrell Schweitzer compared the alzabo feast to
memory experiments on flatworms. All known only through Wright; cite them as Wright cites them.

## 3. Threads ledger

Twelve threads. Each unit lists which are in play. Tags are used in the unit plan.

### [MEMORY] The perfect memory and the desk where it writes

- **What:** Severian forgets nothing, writes from the far end of his life, and is not reliable.
- **First:** I.1 (the boast, and the Roche/Drotte slip on the same page); I.3, where he realizes he
  is "in some degree insane" and cannot be sure his memories are more than his dreams.
- **Develops:** I.20, a parenthesis puts him at his writing table in the House Absolute; II.1, he
  compares himself to a fly in amber; the logged paradoxes (II.13 vs III.3; III.1 vs III.21); III.27,
  he admits he has forgotten much of a climb; IV.28 opens on "today," his last day before leaving
  the House Absolute.
- **Pays off:** IV.38. He will send this manuscript to Master Ultan, write it out again from memory
  aboard ship, and set the second copy adrift. The book in our hands may be the copy.
- **Carry forward:** Is a memory that keeps everything a better witness, or a worse one?

### [THECLA] The self that becomes two, then a thousand

- **What:** Thecla's mind lives in Severian after the feast; later the old Autarch's chorus does.
- **First:** I.6, Ultan's aside about sharing the mind of a dead historian; I.7, Thecla arrives.
- **Develops:** II.10–11, the alzabo feast; II.21, her memories "stir" when the androgyne laughs;
  III.11, he has to work not to speak with her voice; IV.18, he says they are now one person; IV.27,
  he is two in one body, the Autarch a thousand.
- **Pays off:** IV.29–30. He swallows the dying Autarch's forebrain with the drug and hears his own
  voice as that of "Severian, the young torturer."
- **Carry forward:** When Severian chooses mercy, who is choosing?

### [SENTENCE] Obedience, sentence and mercy

- **What:** The torturer's ethic, and Severian's slow move from executing judgment to passing it.
- **First:** I.10, he is sworn to the guild's secret and says he has broken that oath; I.12, the
  knife in Thecla's cell.
- **Develops:** I.29, the "false routine" he teaches Agilus and the one lie he thinks the Pancreator
  might justify; II.4, Morwenna on the scaffold; III.4–8 and III.12, the archon's quiet commission and
  what Severian does with Cyriaca; IV.11, the Ascian's tale of the Just Man; IV.14, Severian admits a
  horror of judging.
- **Pays off:** III.31 reveals the guild's secret: torturers obey. IV.18, the story contest is never
  judged. IV.34, Malrubius's account of a "conservation of justice." IV.36, the new Autarch plans an
  order forbidding torture and means to lock the guild below its own tower for a time.
- **Carry forward:** Is mercy obedience to a higher order, or the end of obedience?

### [CLAW] Relic, gem, thorn

- **What:** The Claw of the Conciliator, its powers, its failures, and what it is.
- **First:** I.4 (as a figure of speech); I.18, the Pelerines' altar is destroyed.
- **Develops:** I.22–23, Dorcas comes out of the lake while the Claw is unknown in his sabretache;
  I.31, he finds it and guesses Agia hid it there; II.13, the uhlan revives and Jonas says he was
  never dead; II.28, it fails Jolenta; III.8, the sick children; III.11, Dorcas tells him the Claw
  raised her; III.24, it fails the boy.
- **Pays off:** III.38, the sapphire shatters and a black, shining claw is inside; IV.2, the dead
  soldier; IV.8, it flickers out over the whole lazaret; IV.31, a thorn on the shore.
- **Carry forward:** Does the power belong to the Claw, to Severian, or to the people around him?

### [FAMILY] The family nobody names

- **What:** The orphan's parents and grandparents are in the book, unannounced.
- **First:** I.2, the old boatman who pulls him from the river asks "Not a woman?"; I.11, the silent
  dark woman in the Katharine ceremony who vanishes each year.
- **Develops:** I.22, the same old man fishes the lake for his drowned wife, Cas; I.25, the waiter's
  note; I.28, Dorcas cannot remember; II.22, her dream of the boat; III.10–11, the lead shot.
- **Pays off:** IV.32, Dorcas at the old man's bier in their ruined shop; IV.37, Ouen, the waiter,
  names his mother Cas and his lost love Catherine, a runaway from an order of monials.
- **Carry forward:** Why does a man who tells everything leave this for the reader to assemble?

### [FACE] The face of power

- **What:** The Autarch, androgynous and unnamed, who keeps appearing in disguise.
- **First:** I.3, the face on Vodalus's coin is androgynous and is not Vodalus.
- **Develops:** I.9, the host of the House Azure; II.20–21, the androgyne in the picture room, whom
  the narrator suddenly calls "the Autarch"; IV.23–24, the pilot of the flier.
- **Pays off:** IV.24–29, the reveal and the succession; IV.31, Malrubius says the predecessor took
  the test and failed, and the stated penalty for failure is a man's manhood; IV.38, the Autarch "smiled in the House Azure" because he had been
  told of Severian.
- **Carry forward:** What does it mean that the ruler has no fixed face, and that so few know it?

### [SUN] The dying sun, the ships, and other times

- **What:** The science fiction under the fantasy furniture.
- **First:** I.1–I.4, the dimming sun and a Citadel of metal towers; I.5, the moon-landing painting.
- **Develops:** I.21, a jungle hut whose people seem to belong to another age; I.35, Hethor's talk of
  star sails; II.16, Jonas's metal body and his ship; III.6, Cyriaca's tale of the made beings who
  returned humanity's wildness; III.24–26, Typhon's carved mountain; III.33–34, the hierodules;
  IV.15–17, the Last House across geological time.
- **Pays off:** IV.30–31, the White Fountain and the test; IV.34, the history of the beings who
  shape humanity "as they themselves were shaped."
- **Carry forward:** Is this fantasy that turns out to be science fiction, or science fiction that
  turns out to be sacred?

### [STAGE] Plays, masks and powers above the stage

- **What:** Theater as Wolfe's figure for hidden direction.
- **First:** I.11, the masking ceremony; I.32, Severian acts in Dr. Talos's play.
- **Develops:** II.23–24, "Eschatology and Genesis"; III.5–7, the masque at the archon's palace;
  III.34, Famulimus's mask over a mask.
- **Pays off:** IV.30, Malrubius calls himself one of the "powers from above the stage" and tells
  Severian he is an actor too.
- **Carry forward:** Who is directing, and does it reduce what the actors choose? (Wright's frame.)

### [AGIA] Agia and the economy of revenge

- **What:** The antagonist who keeps returning, and the one person who refuses forgiveness.
- **First:** I.17–18, the rag shop and the coach.
- **Develops:** I.26–29, the duel and Agilus; II.2–7, the forged letter and the mine; III.15–16, the
  alzabo's house.
- **Pays off:** IV.25, "the mercy of Agia"; IV.37, Severian reckons what Agia, Dorcas and he were to
  each other.
- **Carry forward:** What does revenge look like in a book about mercy?

### [TALES] Stories inside the story

- **What:** The brown book, the plays and the contest, each a lens on the frame.
- **First:** I.6–7, among the books Severian fetches from Ultan's library for Thecla is the brown
  book he will carry away from her cell (named as such at III.6); I.32, the troupe's play.
- **Develops:** II.17, the Tale of the Student and His Son; II.24, the play's script; III.6, Cyriaca's
  tale; III.19, the Tale of the Boy Called Frog.
- **Pays off:** IV.6–18, four soldiers' tales in a judging contest, then copied into the brown book.
- **Carry forward:** What does each embedded story say about the man reading it aloud?

### [WORDS] Words, names and the translator

- **What:** Real but rare words, saints' names, and the pose of a translator from the future.
- **First:** I.1, "fuligin," "chrisos," "exultant"; I. Appendix, the translator says such words are
  "suggestive rather than definitive."
- **Develops:** II. Appendixes on rank, money and time; III. Appendix on provincial government;
  the Ascians, mentioned from I.3 and met at IV.5 (Wolfe: "people without shadows").
- **Pays off:** IV.38, Severian names his book after the lost scripture said to foretell the New Sun.
- **Carry forward:** When is a word doing work in the story, and when is it a rabbit hole?

### [REALM] Vodalus, the Commonwealth and the war

- **What:** The politics of a stagnant state (Wolfe's Byzantium) and Severian's loyalty to a rebel.
- **First:** I.1, the necropolis and the coin; I.33, Malrubius's seven kinds of attachment.
- **Develops:** II.9–11, Vodalus's court in the forest; II.21, the Autarch orders Severian to kill
  the Autarch; III. Appendix; IV.1–IV.22, the war against the Ascians.
- **Pays off:** IV.26–27, Severian as Vodalus's prisoner; IV.29, the throne.
- **Carry forward:** Could the old order be saved, and is Severian its savior or its last servant?

## 4. Volume arcs

### Volume I, *The Shadow of the Torturer* (35 chapters, 98,978 words with appendix)

**What it does in the whole:** It lays down nearly every clue the tetralogy will cash, under cover
of a coming-of-age story and a city adventure. The first twelve chapters are, by Wolfe's account,
the novella the book started as: a torturer, a prisoner, a knife.

**Shape:** Two halves. The Citadel (I.1–14) is closed, ritual and nearly static: a boy's world of
corridors, an archive, a cell, a feast. The city (I.15–35) is a few crowded days and nights,
moving through inns, shops, gardens, a dueling field and a scaffold to the Wall, gathering the
traveling party (Talos, Baldanders, Agia, Dorcas, Jolenta, Hethor). It ends at a gate, with the
first of four farewells to the reader.

**Teaching emphasis:** How to read this narrator. Plant the habits early: note when he writes from
later, note every "I forget nothing," keep a list of strangers and of small impossibilities. The
volume's argument is that mercy and guilt arrive together (Thecla) and that the world is much older
than it looks.

### Volume II, *The Claw of the Conciliator* (31 chapters, 95,748 words with appendixes)

**What it does:** It opens the self. The feast puts Thecla inside Severian, the Claw starts working
in ways neither he nor the reader can pin down, and the House Absolute shows that power hides behind
mirrors and paintings. It is the volume of doubles: twins, false Theclas, a mirror-book, a man who is
partly machine, a play that retells the world.

**Shape:** Three movements. Saltus (II.1–9): a fair town, an execution, a forged letter, a mine.
The forest and the House Absolute (II.10–23): the feast, the antechamber, Jonas's departure, the
Secret House. The play and the road north (II.24–31): "Eschatology and Genesis," the troupe's
breakup, Jolenta's decline, the stone town. It opens with an elision (the fight at the Piteous Gate
told only as a dream) and closes at the edge of the mountains.

**Teaching emphasis:** Who is speaking now; what is a miracle. This is where Evenson's "we behind
the I" earns its keep, and where the hosts should begin keeping score of the Claw.

### Volume III, *The Sword of the Lictor* (38 chapters, 95,748 words with appendix)

**What it does:** It tests the office. Severian finally holds the post he was sentenced to, as
lictor of Thrax, and walks away from it over a single act of mercy. Then the book strips him: of
Dorcas, of a son, of his sword, of the Claw's sapphire. It is the volume where myth becomes
machinery (a carved mountain, a two-headed tyrant, a giant's laboratory, aliens in masks).

**Shape:** Thrax (III.1–12), the mountains with little Severian (III.13–24), Typhon and the lake
(III.25–31), Baldanders's castle (III.32–38). Most action of any volume, and, as Andre-Driussi notes,
almost none of the victories are heroic.

**Teaching emphasis:** Justice in practice, and loss. The structural lesson: Severian withholds the
outcome of the Cyriaca episode for five chapters (III.7–12) and tells it to Dorcas, not to us.

### Volume IV, *The Citadel of the Autarch* (38 chapters, 98,325 words; this text has no appendix)

**What it does:** It explains, partly, and judges. War, a lazaret full of stories, a house outside
time, a battle, the old Autarch unmasked, the succession, Malrubius's revelations, and the return to
the Citadel, where the family is finally assembled and the narrator steps into the present tense.
Wolfe told McCaffery it was his favorite volume.

**Shape:** The lazaret and its stories (IV.1–18), the war (IV.19–24), capture and succession
(IV.25–31), homecoming (IV.32–38). The published volume has an appendix on the Autarch's arms and
the hierodules' ships; it is missing from our text, so check Andrew's edition.

**Teaching emphasis:** The verdict, and the hosts' own. The lazaret contest asks Severian to judge
and he never does; the last chapters ask the listener to judge Severian. Wright and Aramini should
both get a full hearing here, and so should Auerbach's objection.

### Unit 1 — I.1–7: "Resurrection and Death" to "The Traitress" (23,567 words)

**Title: The Locked Gate** · known_through: I.7

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Meet the narrator before the story.* Severian tells us on the first page that he writes long
   after these events and has "backed into the throne." He tells us he forgets nothing, and within
   a page of saying so he gives the same remark to Roche and then to Drotte. Teach the listener to
   hear both voices: the apprentice who sees, and the old man who arranges.
2. *Read the necropolis scene as a first sentence.* A rebel exultant, his beautiful companion, a
   corpse pulled from a grave, a man killed with his own ax, and a coin. Severian chooses a side by
   instinct and spends three chapters doubting his own memory of it (I.3), finally checking the coin
   and finding that its face is androgynous and is not Vodalus's.
3. *See that this is a very old world.* The sun is dim; the Citadel's towers are of "unsmeltable" metal and
   Malrubius woke the boys by drumming on a bulkhead (I.2); the picture cleaner is working on a painting of an armored figure in a
   desert with a stiff banner and a gold visor (I.5). Let the listener recognize the moon landing
   for himself and notice how Severian sees it.
4. *Meet the guild as an institution.* Clients, coffles, the oubliette, an apprentice's chores and
   affections (Triskele, I.4). The torturers are ordinary working men; that is the horror and the
   point.

**Threads in play:** MEMORY, FACE, SUN, REALM, WORDS, FAMILY (the boatman, quietly), THECLA (I.6–7).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The androgynous face on the coin (I.3) is likely the Autarch's (*a strong reading*: no volume says so outright): the host of the House Azure (I.9) and the
  androgyne of II.20–21, revealed at IV.23–24.
- "Backed into the throne" (I.1) is literal by IV.29.
- The boatman who asks "Not a woman?" (I.2) is fishing for his drowned wife, Cas (I.22). She is
  Dorcas, and he is Severian's grandfather (IV.32, IV.37). One Waggish commenter spotted it on a
  second reading.
- The undine who pushed him up from the river (I.2) is explained at IV.38, where Severian says the
  undine thrust him up because those who walk time had seen the first Severian take the throne.
- The symbols over his mausoleum, a fountain, a flying ship and a rose (I.2): Andre-Driussi reads
  them as the first Severian's arms (IV.38 and Chapter Guide Postlude 1). *A community theory.*
- Valeria in the Atrium of Time (I.4) is the woman he sends for at IV.38.
- Triskele (I.4), revived by the boy, returns with Malrubius in visions (I.33) and as an aquastor
  (IV.30–31). At IV.18 Severian supposes a dire wolf killed the dog (and then says it "wasn't really dead"), which is the hinge of
  Andre-Driussi's "resurrect only what you kill."
- Ultan's remark about sharing a dead historian's mind (I.6) prepares the feast (II.10–11).
- The Book of Gold (I.6) returns at IV.33, where Severian wonders whether he has written one.
- The moon picture: Evenson notes that Severian's odd wish to carry it to a mountain forest makes
  sense once the moon is known to be forested and green. *The text says* Lune is green; Evenson's
  point is a reading of tone.

**Critical frame:** Gaiman's first two rules, said aloud at the start of the series. Then Wright on
the mnemonist and on plot versus story; Andre-Driussi's Paradoxes to Perfect Memory for the
Roche/Drotte slip. Dirda 1983 for Wolfe's two starting wishes: the grave-robbing scene and a torturer
seen from inside.

### Unit 2 — I.8–14: "The Conversationalist" to "Terminus Est" (18,398 words)

**Title: A Knife Under the Door** · known_through: I.14

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Understand what Severian did.* He loves a prisoner sentenced to torture, watches her put to the
   question on the revolutionary (I.12), gives her a knife, and then reports himself. Teach the act
   as mercy, crime and confession at once, and listen to how carefully he narrates it: he says he did
   not hear her throat rattle; he saw the blood.
2. *See the guild's ritual and the guild's secret.* The Feast of Holy Katharine (I.11): the wheel, the
   maid, the tinsel sword, the masking. At I.10 he tells us he was sworn to the guild's central secret
   and has since broken the oath. The listener should hold that promise.
3. *Notice doubles and substitutes.* The House Azure (I.9) supplies a false Thecla and a woman made up
   as her sister. The young man meets the counterfeit before he meets the cost.
4. *Watch mercy and justice collide in the institution.* Palaemon explains that the guild may not
   kill Severian without a judicial order, and cannot go to the judges without disgrace (I.13). The
   post of carnifex in Thrax is the compromise, and Palaemon's gift of
   Terminus Est is "a gift" precisely because it is undeserved (I.14).

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, THECLA, STAGE, FACE, FAMILY, MEMORY.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The guild's secret is told at III.31: torturers obey, and will do the unthinkable in obedience.
  Severian tells it to explain what he does on Lake Diuturna.
- The maid of the Katharine ceremony, dark, unchanged, vanishing each year (I.11): IV.37 has Ouen's
  lost Catherine, a runaway from an order of monials. Clute, Feeley and Andre-Driussi read her as
  Severian's mother. *A strong reading.* Aramini notes that the name Gildas, dropped here, is a saint
  tied to a legend of a beheaded woman restored, which matches the ceremony.
- The host of the House Azure (I.9) is the Autarch (IV.23–24); IV.38 says he smiled there because he
  had been told of Severian.
- Malrubius at the door (I.11), dead for years: the first sign of the aquastors (IV.30–31).
- The knife, the blood and the confession come straight from Wolfe's original novella plan, quoted by
  Andre-Driussi from *The Castle of the Otter*. In the plan, a letter from Thecla follows; compare the
  forged letter at II.5.
- Thecla's sentence to the revolutionary: at IV.31 Severian is still remembering her "writhing under
  the Revolutionary."

**Critical frame:** Crampton on torture and confession (the clients, the guild of confessors, the
Katharine liturgy). McCaffery: Wolfe wanted Severian to face the problem of Thecla and the problem of
torture, and to consider that being an agent of pain might not be simply evil. Andre-Driussi's
"Feast of Saint Katharine with a K" for the novella buried in these chapters.

### Unit 3 — I.15–22: "Baldanders" to "Dorcas" (22,148 words)

**Title: The Altar and the Lake** · known_through: I.22

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Follow the city as a gallery of strangers.* A giant and a fox-faced doctor in one bed (I.15), a
   rag shop run by a woman and a corpse-faced man (I.16–17), a challenge delivered as a black seed, a
   pilgrim's cloak. Ask of each stranger what they want from Severian.
2. *Hold the destruction of the altar in mind.* Agia's coach crashes through the Pelerines' cathedral
   (I.18); they search her and say their relic can vanish on its own. Plant the question: where did
   it go?
3. *Read the Botanic Gardens as rooms of time.* The Jungle Garden's hut (I.21) holds a couple and a
   diviner who seem to belong to another age; the Lake of Birds (I.22) keeps the dead and an old man
   who has fished forty years for his wife. Severian goes under. Something holds his hand.
4. *Note what the narrator lets slip.* At I.20 he says he is writing at his table in the House
   Absolute, and he retells Thecla's childhood story of Father Inire's mirrors. Two clues to the
   frame in one chapter.

**Threads in play:** AGIA, CLAW, SUN, FAMILY, MEMORY, TALES, STAGE.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- Agia hid the Claw in Severian's sabretache when the altar broke; he guesses as much at I.31.
- The yellow-haired girl from the lake (I.23) is Dorcas, raised while the unknown Claw was on him. She
  tells him so at III.11. She is also the old man's Cas (I.22, IV.32, IV.37).
- Dr. Talos is Baldanders's homunculus, not his master (III.33); at IV.32 Severian says he overlooked
  a hundred clues that Baldanders was the master. This inn scene holds several.
- The dream in Baldanders's bed of flying and of the undines (I.15) recurs at IV.25, changed:
  Andre-Driussi logs it as the "Paradox of the Vision Dream." *A community theory* that the dream
  shows the first Severian's life.
- The jungle hut is in Andre-Driussi's reading a 20th-century mission (the mail plane, a Zulu title
  for a diviner); Wright and the Chapter Guide treat the gardens as places where time is held or bent,
  as the Last House is (IV.15–17).
- Father Inire's mirrors (I.20) are the same technology that sends Jonas to the stars (II.18).

**Critical frame:** Wright's memory palace (each new site is a distinct locus) and his warning about
deflective allusion. Gordon's labyrinth. Andre-Driussi on the hut.

### Unit 4 — I.23–29: "Hildegrin" to "Agilus" (18,455 words)

**Title: The Sanguinary Field** · known_through: I.29

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Watch Dorcas arrive without a past.* She comes out of the Lake of Birds with no story and no
   memory, and when asked about the note she can only say she does not remember (I.28).
   Let the listener collect what is odd about her.
2. *Read the note at the Inn of Lost Loves (I.25) as evidence.* A warning about the woman with him,
   a remark that he is a torturer, and a last line addressed to someone who looks like a mother come
   again. Ask who wrote it, to whom, and why Agia throws it over the rail.
3. *Treat the duel as a death and a rising.* Severian is struck by the avern and gets up (I.27).
   The chapter title asks the question; the text does not answer it.
4. *See the craft of the headsman.* Agilus, the rag-shop man, is Agia's twin. Severian visits him the
   night before and teaches him the "false routine" that lets the condemned die with less fear, the
   one lie he thinks the Pancreator might justify (I.29). Compassion and professionalism are the same
   gesture here.

**Threads in play:** FAMILY, AGIA, CLAW, SENTENCE, MEMORY, WORDS.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The note's author is Ouen, the waiter (named at I.26), Dorcas's son and Severian's father; he
  recites it word for word at IV.37.
- Hildegrin, Vodalus's man (Severian recognizes his voice at I.24), dies at the stone town (II.31).
- Wright reads the Agilus execution as Wolfe's main device for making a torturer sympathetic: the
  reader is drawn into Severian's dark humor about the bolting sumpter (I.31). Use that in the next
  unit too.
- Was Severian dead on the field? Aramini's review of the Chapter Guide asks the same thing and notes
  how later resurrections leave a body behind. Leave it open even in the tail.

**Critical frame:** Wright, ch. 7, on first-person sympathy (Agilus as his worked example).
Andre-Driussi's commentary on I.27 (the "paschal egg" line) as a small model of how allusion works.

### Unit 5 — I.30–35 and Appendix: "Night" to "Hethor" (16,410 words)

**Title: What the Plowman Sees** · known_through: I.35 and the Appendix

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Close the first act on the scaffold.* The execution of Agilus (I.31) is ceremony and farce at
   once, and the chapter gives the volume its title. Then Severian finds a gem in his sabretache and
   names it: the Claw of the Conciliator.
2. *Take Severian's three meanings seriously (I.32).* Practical, reflective, transubstantial: what
   the plowman sees, what the soothsayer reads, what the Pancreator wills. It is a theory of reading
   offered by the narrator, and the course will test it.
3. *Hear Malrubius's catechism (I.33).* Seven kinds of political attachment, from loyalty to a
   monarch's person up to an abstraction; Severian says his own attachment to the divine is of the
   first kind, "because there is no succession." Note too the "free artist" passage: the executioner
   must add one touch of his own.
4. *Read the Appendix as part of the fiction.* A translator claims a manuscript from the future and
   says his odd words are substitutes, "suggestive rather than definitive." Ask what kind of book
   presents itself that way.

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, CLAW, STAGE, REALM, WORDS, TALES, SUN (Hethor).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The great building that hangs over the city and vanishes (I.31) is what Andre-Driussi calls the
  "flying cathedral": the Pelerines' cathedral, seen in the air. Hildegrin later claims to have followed the party from the Sanguinary Field through the play
  (II.31).
- Hethor, first seen among the five waiting at Agilus's prison (I.30), becomes Agia's instrument and
  the keeper of off-world monsters (III.15, IV.6); his stammered talk of star-sailing ships is literal.
- "No succession" (I.33): the Autarchy passes by the method of IV.29.
- Wright's chapter 10 reads the "free artist" passage as Wolfe's clue to his own game.
- The three meanings: Gordon maps them onto medieval allegory; Wright argues only the plowman's
  meaning leads to the story. Make this the first real host disagreement of the series.

**Critical frame:** Wright vs. Gordon on I.32. Wright on the Appendix (it "destabilises" the words).
The "slingshot ending" (Clute and Robinson's term, via Gevers), and the first of the three
"Here I pause" farewells.

### Unit 6 — II.1–9: "The Village of Saltus" to "The Liege of Leaves" (24,691 words)

**Title: A Fly in Amber** · known_through: II.9

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Notice the gap between volumes.* *Claw* opens on a dream that runs together Morwenna's face, the
   fight at the Piteous Gate, Dorcas's whipped cheek and Malrubius. The party has been scattered and
   Jonas has joined Severian, and none of it is narrated. Teach elision as a technique.
2. *Watch the torturer at work in public.* Saltus's fair, the walled-up Barnoch (II.1–2), Morwenna
   and the cattle thief on the scaffold (II.4), Eusebia's roses. Severian tells us he will not
   describe his other professional occasions. Ask why this one.
3. *Follow the forged letter (II.5–7).* A letter in Thecla's voice lures him to a mine, where the
   Claw lights the dark, the man-apes scatter from something far below, and Agia waits outside. He
   understands she wrote it because he had told her about Thecla.
4. *See loyalty's price.* Seized by Vodalus's men, Severian finally stands before the rebel he served
   as a boy, and admits to the reader that he lied to him about Barnoch (II.9).

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, AGIA, CLAW, REALM, MEMORY, THECLA, SUN (the green man, II.3).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The forged letter echoes Wolfe's abandoned novella, in which a real letter from Thecla was the
  crisis (Andre-Driussi, quoting *Castle of the Otter*).
- Severian's line "That we are capable only of being what we are remains our unforgivable sin" opens
  and closes this volume (II.1, II.31). Wright reads it as the lament of the whole cycle.
- The man-ape whose hand Severian cut off is healed by the Claw; at IV.10 he lists it among cases where
  he caused the harm. Andre-Driussi, Postlude 3.
- The thing below the mine: the Chapter Guide links the thundering steps in the mine to the walking tower
  of the war (*Citadel*). *A community theory.*
- The green man in the showman's tent (II.3), freed with half a whetstone, is a man from the future;
  Severian alludes to his escape down a corridor of time at IV.31.

**Critical frame:** Crampton on Morwenna's execution as historical practice (the gallows speech, the
branding, the religious and civil officers). Andre-Driussi's Paradoxes for the pistol in II.1.

### Unit 7 — II.10–16: "Thea" to "Jonas" (19,667 words)

**Title: The Banquet of the Dead** · known_through: II.16

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Face the feast directly.* Thea explains the alzabo drug (II.10); Severian and Jonas understand
   what will be eaten. In II.11 Severian vomits, eats, and takes Thecla into himself; the chapter ends
   in a vision of the two of them as one. Discuss it as communion inverted and as chemistry, and let
   the hosts disagree.
2. *Track the new voice.* From here on the listener should hear for Thecla in the narration: her
   memories, her rank, her habits.
3. *Test the Claw.* A notule kills an uhlan; Severian lays the Claw on him and he lives (II.13).
   Jonas says he was never quite dead. The book gives us both accounts and picks neither.
4. *Meet the antechamber (II.14–16).* Prisoners kept for generations outside the House Absolute,
   forgotten by those who put them there, and Jonas revealed as partly metal, a sailor from a ship
   that came home to an Urth with no port.

**Threads in play:** THECLA, CLAW, REALM, SUN, SENTENCE, MEMORY.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The steel given at the feast (II.11), Vodalus's token, is the message the androgyne reads at II.21.
- At IV.10 Severian says the Claw helped Jonas because "I—Thecla—had used those whips," which
  implies Thecla once used them herself. Evenson reads a parenthesis in *Claw*, where Severian
  remembers laughing with friends among instruments of torture, as Thecla's memory surfacing; he
  infers she and her friends tormented prisoners for amusement. *A strong reading.*
- Andre-Driussi ("Posthistory 201") reads Jonas as the book's man of history: he knew the Citadel hill
  as a port and Nessus before it had that name, and he may have found the descendants of an old
  shipmate among the antechamber's prisoners. *A community theory.*
- Wright, ch. 6: the alzabo's union is chemical, not mystical. At IV.10 the Pelerine Ava proposes
  instead that the Claw, carried unknowing at the feast, did bring Thecla back. Two explanations
  again.

**Critical frame:** Evenson ("a 'we' lurking behind the 'I'"). Wright, ch. 6, on "rationalised
fantasy." Palmer and Mirus for the Eucharistic reading; label it.

### Unit 8 — II.17–23: "The Tale of the Student and His Son" to "Jolenta" (22,861 words)

**Title: The Secret House** · known_through: II.23

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Read the first embedded tale for what it says about the frame.* Severian reads aloud from
   Thecla's brown book to a silent Jonas (II.17): a student who must make a dream son, a son who
   sails to fight a monster in a maze, black sails misread as mourning. Ask what it says about making
   people, and about misreading signs.
2. *Watch Jonas leave.* He goes through Father Inire's mirrors (II.18). Severian is alone for the
   first time since the city.
3. *Explore the House Absolute as a deception.* A palace hidden underground and in its gardens, a
   room that poses as a painting, and an androgyne who reads Vodalus's message, shows Severian a book
   of mirror pages, and tells him to restore the Claw to the Pelerines, go to Thrax, and in time kill
   the Autarch (II.20–21). Severian sweats blood. Then the narrator calls the man "the Autarch."
4. *Return to the troupe.* Dorcas's dreams of the boat and the black water (II.22); Jolenta's beauty
   and its cost (II.23); a program for a play.

**Threads in play:** FACE, TALES, STAGE, FAMILY, THECLA, SUN, REALM.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The androgyne is the Autarch (IV.23–24), ordering his own assassination in Vodalus's name. The slip
  in II.21 is the old narrator's knowledge breaking into the young man's scene.
- The winged figure above the mirror pages, part woman and part butterfly, wings marked with eyes:
  readers commonly link it to the shapers Malrubius describes (IV.34). *A community theory.*
- Dorcas's dream (II.22) is her death and burial in the lake; III.10–11 and IV.32 confirm it.
- Andre-Driussi reads the Student's tale as Theseus crossed with Vance's "Turjan of Miir," a story
  about synthetic people; Dr. Talos is one (III.33).
- Palmer reads the haematidrosis as the crown of thorns and the eclipse carved on the cabinet as
  Severian's death. *A community theory.*

**Critical frame:** Wright, ch. 5 ("The Toy Theatre"): the House Absolute as stage machinery. Gordon
on embedded tales as paths into the maze; Wright's reply that they lead off to the side.

### Unit 9 — II.24–31 and Appendixes: "Dr. Talos's Play" to "The Cleansing" (28,529 words)

**Title: Eschatology and Genesis** · known_through: II.31 and Appendixes

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Read the play as a map.* "Eschatology and Genesis," billed as drawn from the lost Book of the New
   Sun (II.24): Gabriel, the first man and woman, an Autarch, a torturer, a giant who breaks his
   chains. The title runs backwards. Ask what the play predicts and who wrote it.
2. *See the performance break.* Baldanders turns the stage into an assault on the hierodules in the
   audience (II.25); the troupe scatters; Dr. Talos and Baldanders part from Severian (II.26).
3. *Follow Jolenta's decline as a judgment on artifice.* Made beautiful by the doctor, she fails
   without his drugs; the undine of Abaia rises from the river for Severian (II.27–28); the Claw does
   nothing for Jolenta. Severian discovers he loves her only when he pities her.
4. *Stand in the stone town (II.31).* The Cumaean, Merryn, Hildegrin and a summoning of a man from the
   deep past, Apu-Punchau; a fight, a crack of thunder, and two of the party gone. The volume ends
   with the line it began with.

**Threads in play:** STAGE, TALES, CLAW, SUN, REALM, AGIA (through Hildegrin), WORDS (Appendixes).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The three hierodules at the play are Ossipago, Barbatus and Famulimus (III.33–34); they shot
  Baldanders when he went berserk (Chapter Guide).
- The play's giant breaking free while the torturer works: Palmer reads Baldanders as the Antichrist.
  *A community theory.* The fight in the bailey (III.35–37) is the real version.
- Apu-Punchau is, by Severian's own guess at IV.38, the first Severian at the end of his journey.
  Cyriaca's tale of the stone town (III.7) and the Pelerine cape left in the mud tie it to the Pelerines.
- The Cumaean was "not a woman at all," something sleekly reptilian (II.31). At IV.35 Father Inire's
  letter returns to the theme of sibyls; Andre-Driussi links it to the Sibyl of Cumae.
- The Appendixes describe the Commonwealth's seven ranks: a society frozen by caste, which is Wolfe's
  Byzantium (McCaffery).

**Critical frame:** Wright on the play as the outline of the Hierogrammates' drama; Andre-Driussi on
its Zoroastrian and biblical sources (Meschia, Meschiane, Jahi, the Nephilim). The "slingshot ending"
again for the close of *Claw*.

### Unit 10 — III.1–8: "Master of the House of Chains" to "Upon the Cliff" (20,367 words)

**Title: The City of Windowless Rooms** · known_through: III.8

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *See the office from the inside.* Severian is lictor at last, running the Vincula (III.1–4), and
   good at it. Dorcas cannot bear the town's name for him and falls silent (III.1–3).
2. *Weigh the archon's commission.* Abdiesus wants a woman of his court removed quietly, without the
   public spectacle executions normally are (III.4; see the III Appendix on how archons govern). It is the first sentence
   Severian is asked to carry out in secret.
3. *Meet Cyriaca at the masque (III.5–7).* A former Pelerine, unfaithful, frightened, telling a
   story about the lost archives of Nessus that turns out to be his own Citadel library seen from far
   away, and a tale of made beings who returned humanity's wild half to ruin their makers.
4. *Notice what the narrator withholds.* The archon finds them (III.7). The next chapter follows
   Severian into the night and heals children in a hut, and does not say what happened to her.

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, THECLA, CLAW, SUN, STAGE, WORDS.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- Severian lets Cyriaca go downriver (III.12), paying for Thecla's life with hers, and must flee
  Thrax. He tells it to Dorcas, not to us, five chapters later.
- Palmer guesses that Cyriaca is Severian's mother, Catherine; Clute and Andre-Driussi favor the
  Katharine maid instead (I.11, IV.37). Keep Palmer's as *a community theory*.
- Cyriaca's Pelerine cape and the stone town: the same cape Severian picked up at II.31.
- The children's healing (III.8): Andre-Driussi calls it ambiguous, perhaps the first sign of
  Severian's power over disease.

**Critical frame:** Crampton (private execution as a break from historical form, and the archon's
embarrassment). Andre-Driussi's Chapter Guide on "eisegesis" in Cyriaca's tale (III.6).

### Unit 11 — III.9–16: "The Salamander" to "The Alzabo" (20,539 words)

**Title: What Dorcas Knew** · known_through: III.16

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Hear Dorcas's discovery (III.10–11).* She vomits lead shot, recognizes a chair from a ruined
   house, and works out what the dead in the Lake of Birds were weighted with. She tells Severian the
   Claw brought her back. He pushes back; she knows.
2. *Get the Cyriaca outcome, told late (III.11–12).* Severian narrates it to Dorcas. Ask why the
   memoir delays it and changes its audience.
3. *Read the parting.* Dorcas goes south to find her past; Severian goes north as a fugitive. He tells
   us it will be a long time before he sees her again.
4. *Enter the mountains.* Casdoe's house, Agia's return, and the alzabo, which calls to the family in the
   voices of those it has already eaten (III.14–16). The feast of II.11 seen from the
   animal's side.

**Threads in play:** FAMILY, CLAW, SENTENCE, AGIA, THECLA, SUN (the salamander).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- Dorcas's past is the shop by the river and the old man's body on the bier (IV.32).
- The salamander and the alzabo are Hethor's creatures from off-world (Hethor named at III.12 and
  IV.6).
- Andre-Driussi's Paradox: Dorcas's "You think the Claw brought you back" (III.11) against Dorcas being
  out of the water first (I.23).
- Wright: the alzabo absorbs memories, not souls (III.16 and Severian's later meditation on it at
  III.27). Use as the skeptic's line.

**Critical frame:** Wright, ch. 6 (the alzabo as secular mechanism) against Mirus/Aramini. Evenson
for the alzabo's borrowed voice as a dark mirror of Severian's own.

### Unit 12 — III.17–24: "The Sword of the Lictor" to "The Corpse" (22,446 words)

**Title: Severian and Severian** · known_through: III.24

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Watch Severian become a father.* Casdoe's small son is also named Severian (III.18). The man who
   was raised by a guild raises a boy, for a few days.
2. *Read the Tale of the Boy Called Frog (III.19) beside the boy.* Twins in a basket, a wolf senate,
   a city founded and a brother killed. The boy asks for a story with a big friend and a twin and
   wolves, and gets one.
3. *Follow the sorcerers' duel (III.20–22).* Magicians with steel claws who want to hold back the New
   Sun; a contest won by an intruding monster, not by skill.
4. *Climb the carved mountain (III.22–24).* A ruined town, a two-headed corpse, metal guardsmen who
   watch the sun, and a ring on a giant stone hand that kills the boy in a flash of violet light.
   The Claw does nothing. Severian says that if it had, he would have cut his own throat for Thecla.

**Threads in play:** TALES, CLAW, SUN, FAMILY (by echo), MEMORY.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The two-headed corpse is Typhon, who wakes (III.25); at III.26 he says his ring killed the child.
  Andre-Driussi counts Typhon's waking among the Claw's resurrections. *A community theory.*
- The Frog tale is Romulus and Remus with Kipling's Mowgli (Chapter Guide); Andre-Driussi's Apollo
  postlude links the twin and the wolves to Severian himself, who "has a twin" in the reading that
  makes Merryn or the homunculus of IV.35 his sibling. *A community theory* (Clute's SFE entry says
  "almost certainly" a sibling, candidate unknown).
- Typhon's mountain is the reason later volumes of Wolfe's cycle exist (Wright ch. 1; Gevers).
  **[Urth]** and Long Sun material stays out of the body.

**Critical frame:** Andre-Driussi, Postlude 4 ("Conan the Proustian"): the most action-packed volume
and none of its victories heroic. Wright on the memory palace for the mountain sequence.

### Unit 13 — III.25–31: "Typhon and Piaton" to "The People of the Lake" (16,981 words)

**Title: The Eyes of the World** · known_through: III.31

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Understand the temptation (III.25–26).* Typhon, the ancient monarch living on in a slave's
   body, shows Severian the world from the mountain's eyes, offers to make him steward of it with a
   throne in Nessus, and tells him to view his empire. Severian kills
   Typhon by striking the slave, Piaton, who had asked for death.
2. *See power as a carved mountain.* Typhon explains the sun's decline in a few years of famine and
   riot. The scale of the old empire against the scale of the stars.
3. *Read the lake people.* The hetman's house, a chained girl named Pia, the Claw stolen by a steel-
   beaked bird (III.28), the hetman's boat destroyed with natrium (III.30), the floating islanders.
4. *Receive the guild's secret (III.31).* Severian finally tells us what he was sworn to at I.10:
   the torturers obey. He tells it to explain a night on Lake Diuturna and his willingness to give the
   Increate what he gave the Autarch.

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, REALM, SUN, CLAW, MEMORY.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The guild's secret is the hinge of the whole ethic: at IV.36 he will use obedience to end torture.
- Aramini reads Typhon as the book's Satan figure; the offer of the world from a mountaintop invites
  the comparison with the Gospel temptation, which is the hosts' observation, not his. Gevers and
  Wright trace Typhon into the later series. **[Urth]**/Long Sun stays out of the body.
- "We are like children who look at print and see a serpent in the last letter but one" (III.30):
  a favorite line of Gaiman's, per Andre-Driussi, and a good motto for the reader-as-detective.
- III.27: Severian concedes he forgets things after all. Log it in MEMORY.

**Critical frame:** Wright on intergeneric hybrid (the carved face as both myth and machine); the
Chapter Guide's Joyce note on the wandering rocks (III.30).

### Unit 14 — III.32–38 and Appendix: "To the Castle" to "The Claw" (15,415 words)

**Title: The Black Claw** · known_through: III.38 and Appendix

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Unmask Dr. Talos and his master (III.32–34).* Baldanders made the doctor; three masked visitors
   from the stars bargain with the giant for knowledge. Famulimus removes a mask, then the monstrous face under
   it, which is a second mask, and shows a calm and beautiful face; she calls Severian "Liege,"
   explains that they wear horror so that humans will keep their own rulers, and asks whether the
   world is only a war of good and bad.
2. *Watch the fight in the bailey (III.35–37).* Terminus Est breaks; Baldanders goes into the lake.
3. *Find the Claw's core (III.38).* The sapphire has shattered. Inside is a small black claw that
   gives light and seems to erase thought. Ask what a relic is if its jewel was only a container.
4. *Leave with Severian for the war.* The last "Here I pause."

**Threads in play:** CLAW, STAGE, SUN, REALM, WORDS (Appendix).

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The hierodules' masks and Malrubius's "powers above the stage" (IV.30): Wright's central metaphor.
- The black claw becomes, or is replaced by, a thorn on the southern shore (IV.31), and Severian
  concludes the principle may rest in every thorn. Palmer reads the gem as a pyx.
- Andre-Driussi notes the hierodules spent years at Severian's court before this visit (IV and
  **[Urth]**); keep to what Citadel supports.
- Andre-Driussi logs Severian's misattribution at III.32 of the flying saucer story to Dorcas rather
  than Cyriaca.

**Critical frame:** Wright, ch. 5 (the masks as a revelation of authoritarianism) against Famulimus's
own question, which the hosts should take at face value too.

### Unit 15 — IV.1–9: "The Dead Soldier" to "Melito's Story" (20,890 words)

**Title: The Lazaret** · known_through: IV.9

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Raise the dead soldier (IV.1–3).* Severian reads a dead man's letter, then the man walks beside
   him, and something in his face recalls Jonas. Begin the volume with the most direct use of the Claw.
2. *Enter the lazaret and the war's rumor (IV.4–6).* Pelerines, fever, Malrubius in a vision, and an
   Ascian prisoner who speaks only in quotations of Correct Thought.
3. *Start the contest (IV.6–9).* Foila sets the rules and Severian is the judge. Hallvard's tale of
   brothers and murder; Melito's fable of a cock, an angel and an eagle. Each suitor is courting Foila
   through his story.
4. *Note Severian's failure (IV.8).* He holds up the Claw over all the sick, and it flickers out.

**Threads in play:** CLAW, TALES, SENTENCE, REALM, THECLA, WORDS ("ascian").

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- Severian believes Jonas has entered the dead Miles (IV.6) and says so to him. Andre-Driussi lists it
  as the fifth resurrection case, "Jonas's soul invades."
- Wolfe told McCaffery the Ascians are North Americans and the word means people without shadows.
- The lazaret will be destroyed while Severian is away (IV.18).

**Critical frame:** Andre-Driussi's tale-by-tale genre notes; Gordon on embedded tales; McCaffery
for the Ascians and for Wolfe's statement that the war backdrop prepares Citadel's larger insights.

### Unit 16 — IV.10–18: "Ava" to "Foila's Request" (22,813 words)

**Title: A Contest Without a Judge** · known_through: IV.18

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Hear the Ascian's tale (IV.11).* A just man beaten again and again, told entirely in approved
   phrases and translated by Foila. Ask what language can carry under total control, and what it
   says that he will never give up.
2. *Hear Foila's tale (IV.13)* and Severian's "horror of judging," which he traces to a guild that
   trains its apprentices to carry out the instructions of judges they are not (IV.14). The judge
   postpones.
3. *Go to the Last House (IV.15–17).* Mannea sends Severian to bring an anchorite to safety; the
   house stands in different times, and from its window Severian sees a world under ice. Master Ash
   vanishes as Severian walks him out.
4. *Return to ruin (IV.18).* The lazaret is destroyed. Foila asks him to remember and tell the
   stories, and he has: we have just read them. The contest is never judged.

**Threads in play:** SENTENCE, TALES, SUN, THECLA, MEMORY, CLAW.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The unjudged contest anticipates Severian's own trial on Yesod (IV.31). **[Urth]** describes that
  trial; keep it out.
- Winnoc (IV.12), whipped long ago by Palaemon, tells of the Pelerines. Crampton uses him as the case
  of the lightest punishment.
- IV.18: Severian says he and Thecla are now one person, and describes the Atrium of Time with an
  obelisk of sundials that does not match I.4 (Andre-Driussi's Paradox).
- The Last House: Andre-Driussi and Wright both treat it as time architecture like the Botanic Gardens.

**Critical frame:** Wright's mnemonist (Severian cannot generalize, so cannot judge) against Mirus's
moral growth. Andre-Driussi on Foila's tale as a mirror of her own suitors.

---

### Unit 17 — IV.19–24: "Guasacht" to "The Flier" (17,606 words)

**Title: Two, as People Are Counted** · known_through: IV.24

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Serve in the irregulars (IV.19–21).* Guasacht's half-mercenary company, patrols, and a
   young woman in the ranks who insists she can fight. War as a job.
2. *Read the battle (IV.22) as Wolfe's soldier's-eye view.* Severian says he forgot nothing but saw
   nothing clearly.
3. *Recognize the pilot (IV.23–24).* The man who rescues him is the androgyne of the House Azure and
   the picture room: the Autarch. He tells Severian he is two, as people are counted, and asks how many
   he himself is.
4. *Put together the Autarch's three disguises* and ask what kind of ruler keeps a brothel, a painted
   room and a flier.

**Threads in play:** FACE, REALM, THECLA, MEMORY.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- "How many do you think I am?" is answered at IV.27 and IV.29: a thousand, then a thousand and two.
- Wolfe to McCaffery: he placed Severian in a vast war so the reader would feel how small one soldier
  is, preparing the end of *Citadel*.

**Critical frame:** Wolfe's own account (McCaffery) of the war's purpose; Wright ch. 1 on Wolfe's
Korean War letters and *The Red Badge of Courage* as a model for the march to the front.

### Unit 18 — IV.25–31: "The Mercy of Agia" to "The Sand Garden" (16,979 words)

**Title: Powers Above the Stage** · known_through: IV.31

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Receive Agia's mercy (IV.25).* She pays him back for the mine by leaving him alive. Discuss revenge
   that ends in a gift.
2. *Hear the present tense (IV.28).* "Today" Severian is about to leave the House Absolute; the memoir
   has caught up with its writer. Then back to Vodalus's camp, where Severian is a prisoner (IV.26–27)
   and says he is two in one body and the Autarch a thousand.
3. *Witness the succession (IV.29–30).* The dying Autarch asks Severian to take the drug and swallow
   his forebrain. Severian hears his own voice as someone else's and asks who he is.
4. *Hear Malrubius (IV.30–31).* Powers from above the stage; the White Fountain; a test of the race in
   which the Autarch represents humanity, and a penalty for failure that the predecessor paid. On the
   shore, a thorn.

**Threads in play:** FACE, THECLA, STAGE, SUN, AGIA, REALM, CLAW, SENTENCE.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- IV.34 gives the rest of Malrubius's history, which Severian holds back for three chapters.
- Andre-Driussi uses IV.31 (the penalty prevents bequeathing the throne) against the Catherine-the-Weal
  theory.
- Wright: the whole drama is the Hierogrammates' mythmaking, and Severian's sense of being a star is
  his mnemonist's dissociation. Aramini: it is theodicy. **[Urth]** decides the test; it stays out.

**Critical frame:** This is the unit for the course's main debate. Wright (ch. 5 and ch. 9) and
Aramini, given equal time; Wolfe's "Christian figure" answer (Person); Evenson on building an "I"
out of a thousand.

### Unit 19 — IV.32–38: "The Samru" to "Resurrection" (20,037 words)

**Title: The Last Year of the Old Sun** · known_through: IV.38 (end of course)

**Teaching aims (fenced)**
1. *Close the family (IV.32, IV.37).* Dorcas kneeling at the bier of the old man from the lake, in the
   ruins of their shop; Ouen, the waiter, with a locket of his mother Cas and the memory of Catherine.
   Let the listener say aloud what Severian never quite does.
2. *Weigh the homecoming (IV.33–36).* The Book of Gold and its librarians (IV.33); Palaemon on the sword;
   Malrubius's history of beings shaped as humanity is now being shaped, "a conservation of justice";
   Father Inire's letter; an order to end torture, and a plan to lock the guild below its own tower.
3. *Read the last chapter as the narrator's final evidence (IV.38).* He is "not the first Severian";
   he will send this manuscript to Ultan and write it again from memory aboard ship; he signs as
   Severian the Lame, Autarch.
4. *Deliver the course's verdict.* Severian set out to execute sentences and ends going to be judged.
   The hosts give the case for the sacred reading, the case for the secular one, and Auerbach's
   objection that we cannot judge at all, and let Andrew decide.

**Threads in play:** all twelve.

**Payoff notes (spoilers allowed)**
- The tail of the final episode may preview *The Urth of the New Sun*, if Andrew wants a coda:
  Wright's synopsis (ch. 4) and the Chapter Guide's Volume V cover the trial on Yesod. Offer it as a
  separate optional episode rather than spending it here.
- Clute's family reading complete: Dorcas, the boatman, Ouen, Catherine; a sibling uncertain.
- The three "Here I pause" farewells (I.35, II.31, III.38) end here with "My pen halts, though I do
  not."

**Critical frame:** Wright's conclusion (Wolfe as a Daedalus who wants to be understood); Gaiman's
rules again; Auerbach's dissent; Wolfe's 2002 line that fiction's most important gift is the hope
that things need not be as they are.
