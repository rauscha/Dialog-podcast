JUNO [grounded]: Picture a crowded street. Midday. Someone raises a gun, and the person across from them raises a hand — and the bullet stops. Hangs there.
CASPAR [dry]: We're in a tabletop roleplaying game.
JUNO [yes, and]: We are. *Mage: The Ascension*, 1993, White Wolf — same company that did *Vampire: The Masquerade*. Third game in the World of Darkness line. And it does something the others don't.
CASPAR [waiting]: Which is?
JUNO [landing it]: The bullet stops. The crowd sees it. And then the street pushes back.
CASPAR [beat]: Pushes back how?
JUNO [precise]: The air thickens. Something tears. The person who stopped the bullet — the one who did the impossible thing — is the one who gets hurt. Not the shooter. Not the crowd. The mage.
CASPAR [naming it]: So there's a cost.
JUNO [correcting]: Not a fuel gauge. The game calls it Paradox. And Paradox isn't a punishment from a god or a rule from a rulebook — it's the inertia of what everyone alive believes to be true. Billions of people's worth of *that can't happen*, and you just walked into it.
CASPAR [turning it over]: So reality isn't fixed.
JUNO [exactly]: It's maintained. Collectively. By belief. The laws of physics exist because humanity, at scale, agrees they do. A mage is someone who knows that — and can push against it. But pushing against it in front of witnesses means the witnesses push back.
CASPAR [pressing]: How do you avoid it?
JUNO [the key]: Two ways. Vulgar magic — stopping a bullet mid-air, visibly, in front of people — that triggers Paradox hard. But coincidental magic, things that look like they could plausibly happen on their own? A car that won't start. A wound that heals slightly faster than it should. Reality doesn't notice, or doesn't care, because it doesn't register as impossible.
CASPAR [sitting with it]: So the mechanic is also a political argument. Whoever controls what people think is possible controls what's physically possible.
JUNO [yes]: That's the setup. We'll get there.
CASPAR [moving on]: Someone discovers all of this. What does that feel like?
JUNO [handing off]: That's yours.
CASPAR [taking it]: The game calls it Awakening. And the rulebook is careful — it does not say it's a gift. It says it's the most significant and terrifying event of your life. You wake up one morning and the walls of the room you're sitting in are not solid. They're a story everyone agreed to tell. The floor, the ceiling, the window — all of it is a consensus. A shared hallucination that happens to be load-bearing.
JUNO [quietly]: That's the Matrix.
CASPAR [measured]: Brucato — Phil Brucato, lead developer from the second edition onward, practicing pagan, spent years infusing real occult philosophy into the mechanics — he's said Mage was a major inspiration. The Wachowskis haven't officially confirmed it. The game came out in 1993. The Matrix was 1999. Draw your own line.
JUNO [pressing]: What does Awakening actually cost?
CASPAR [the point]: You can no longer fully live inside the world everyone else inhabits. That's not a side effect — that's the definition. A mage sees through a lie that billions of people are living inside, and now reality will punish them every time they act on what they know. Stewart Wieck originated the game, released it at Gen Con on August 19, 1993. The estrangement was built in from the start.
JUNO [drawn elsewhere]: But there's something inside the mage pushing them forward too — the Avatar. Their inner compass toward whatever the goal is.
CASPAR [yes, complicated]: Every mage has one. A fragment of their soul — or something that presents as one — pushing them toward Ascension. But Avatars have their own agendas. The rulebook describes some of them as pillars of flame, or devils, or whirlwinds. Always hungry. Often frightening. Your guide to enlightenment may be indistinguishable from a demon.
JUNO [wanting liberation]: But the Awakening itself — you see the lie, and you're outside it. There's something in that worth holding.
CASPAR [holding the line]: The game won't let it be purely good. The rupture is real. The freedom is real. The cost is real. Brucato called it possibly the most metaphysically sophisticated game ever created. He didn't call it a happy one.
JUNO [accepting it]: Okay. A person Awakens. They're not alone — there are others. And in 1466, those others made a decision that shaped the next five centuries.
CASPAR [passing it back]: Yours.
JUNO [scene]: A council table. 1466. Practitioners of nine completely different magical traditions — blood witches, death-workers, mind-benders, scholars of the Hermetic arts going back to ancient Alexandria, chaos magicians, tantrikas, and more — sitting down together for the first time. They've been fighting each other for generations. They stop.
CASPAR [why]: What changed?
JUNO [the answer]: The alternative was extinction. There was an enemy, and the enemy was winning. So nine traditions formed the Council of Nine Mystic Traditions — united by a shared theory that magic operates across nine Spheres, each Tradition specializing in different ones. United by a common enemy. Divided by almost everything else.
CASPAR [pressing]: What does divided mean, concretely?
JUNO [not softening it]: One of the nine is called the Euthanatos. They believe death is transformation — that some people are ready to die, and it's their role to help that along. They have historically killed people they judged ready. Another is the Verbena — blood-and-earth witches whose practices historically included human sacrifice.
CASPAR [flat]: These are the protagonists.
JUNO [yes]: These are the protagonists. The moral failures aren't added later as complications — they're in the founding lore. The Traditions fight for a world where magic is possible. That world includes the Euthanatos deciding who is ready to die.
CASPAR [structural]: So if the heroes are this compromised, what does the war's moral weight actually rest on?
JUNO [not resolving it]: That depends entirely on who they're fighting.
CASPAR [beat]: Right. The enemy ended smallpox.
JUNO [handing off]: Go.
CASPAR [scene, no irony]: A hospital in 1900. A child survives smallpox because of a vaccine. The faction that made that possible — vaccines, antibiotics, electricity, surgery, global communication, the predictable world that ordinary people can plan their lives inside — is the game's villain.
JUNO [surrogate]: Why?
CASPAR [the case]: Before 1900, average human lifespan was considerably shorter. Plague, cholera, smallpox — supernatural predators in the World of Darkness, but also just the world. The Technocracy — descended from an organization called the Order of Reason, founded in 1325 — their project was protecting ordinary humans from supernatural predators. Vampires. Werewolves. Reality-warping madmen. They did it through rationalism and technology. They made reality stable and predictable. They mostly won. And the winning looked like modern medicine.
JUNO [the question]: So why are they the villains?
CASPAR [the turn]: Because their magic looks like science. A vaccine doesn't trigger Paradox. Nobody sees a vaccine and thinks *that's impossible*. The Technocracy wins the Ascension War partly by making their miracles look mundane. And in doing so, they've been slowly closing the door on anything that doesn't fit their model. A world where the impossible is impossible. Where human potential is capped at what they permit.
JUNO [pushing]: But that world is also safer. Objectively.
CASPAR [naming discomfort]: The game asks you to root against the faction that ended smallpox. That's not a rhetorical move — that's the actual design. By the 20th Anniversary Edition, the text is explicit: the difference between the Traditions and the Technocracy is ideology and methodology, not good versus evil. The Traditions want a world where magic is real. That world also contains vampires, and demons, and whatever the Euthanatos decide to do on a given Tuesday.
JUNO [sitting with it]: Neither side is clean.
CASPAR [agreed]: Neither side is clean. And then there are the things that don't fit into either side at all.
JUNO [taking it]: Three of them. First: a man walking down a street, and the street is wrong — not for him, for everyone else. His delusions have become more real than the pavement, and reality is bending to match him. The game calls these mages Marauders. Their Avatars have shattered, trapping them inside personal delusions so powerful that reality reorganizes itself around them.
CASPAR [key detail]: And they're immune to Paradox.
JUNO [yes]: Because they've already broken. The collective disbelief of billions of people can't reach them anymore. They're outside the system entirely.
CASPAR [cutting in]: So a Marauder is—
JUNO [resuming]: Either the ultimate expression of Awakening — individual will completely overriding consensus — or it's what Awakening looks like when it destroys you.
CASPAR [flat]: The game doesn't say which.
JUNO [holding both]: No. Second: a woman who looked at the thing inside her soul reaching toward the light — her Avatar — and deliberately turned it the other way. The Nephandi. They've inverted their Avatars. They don't serve Ascension. They serve Oblivion — not death, but erasure. The unmaking of existence itself.
CASPAR [the scary part]: And they're inside every faction.
JUNO [structural paranoia]: The lore suggests the Nephandi may be winning the Ascension War through infiltration. Any mage in the setting might be compromised. Any Tradition member, any Technocracy agent. The game builds that uncertainty in as a permanent condition. You never know.
CASPAR [connecting back]: And the third thing is the Avatar itself. Which we've already said might be indistinguishable from a demon.
JUNO [landing it]: The thing pushing every mage toward Ascension has its own agenda. Which raises the question of what Ascension even is.
CASPAR [the pivot]: Which brings us to the word at the top of a page in the rulebook.
JUNO [passing it]: Yours.
CASPAR [quiet]: The word "Ascension." Written at the top of the page. Nothing defined beneath it. Every mage in this setting is fighting a centuries-long war toward this goal. No mage has ever been confirmed to have achieved it. Every Tradition has a different answer — the Verbena think it's reunion with the living earth, the Virtual Adepts think it's something like transcending the body through information, the Technocracy think it's a perfected rational humanity. The game never adjudicates between them.
JUNO [the question]: Is that an oversight?
CASPAR [working it out]: The rulebook says explicitly that how Ascension is achieved is purposely left undefined. The war has been fought for centuries over a destination nobody can describe. That's either the game's deepest honesty about what transcendence actually is — something that can't be defined in advance, only approached — or it's the most convenient evasion of a question the designers raised and couldn't answer. I genuinely don't know which.
JUNO [finding something]: I keep landing on the honesty reading. A war prosecuted across generations for a prize that has never been defined — that's also just a description of human longing. We don't know what we're reaching toward either. We just reach.
CASPAR [not dismissing it]: Maybe.
JUNO [pressing]: It maps perfectly though —
CASPAR [cutting in]: Or the game promised a destination and left the map blank.
JUNO [beat]: Both feel true from different angles.
CASPAR [dry]: Which is either very sophisticated or very convenient.
JUNO [the turn]: And then in 2005, a second rulebook tried to answer the question with a completely different cosmology.
CASPAR [surrogate]: What changed?
JUNO [the reboot]: *Mage: The Awakening* — same question, different metaphysics, no shared continuity. Full replacement, not a sequel. Consensus reality is gone. Instead: five Supernal Realms, higher planes where magic originates, separated from the ordinary world — the Fallen World — by something called the Abyss. The Abyss was created when the mages of Atlantis climbed toward the Supernal Realms and broke the connection behind them. Their hubris was so catastrophic it erased them from history. Atlantis is now, the text says, little more than a fairy tale.
CASPAR [the factions]: And the war?
JUNO [the new shape]: The Exarchs — mages who ascended and now actively prevent others from doing the same — versus the Oracles, who also ascended but left Watchtowers behind so ordinary humans could still reach magic. CASPAR [the self-revision]: And then Brucato said he got something wrong.
JUNO [yes]: In a later interview — this is a man who called the game the most metaphysically sophisticated ever created, who meant every word of it — he said the first edition's fear of technology was misguided. The game's own lead developer revised his reading of what the engine was arguing.
CASPAR [sitting with it]: Which is either a designer being honest about what thirty years of evidence showed him — or a man revising a game's argument because the argument aged badly and he noticed.
