CASPAR [flat, direct]: The STRASS trial has been in the literature for years. Retroperitoneal sarcoma, preoperative radiotherapy versus surgery alone — abdominal recurrence-free survival, no significant difference. That result has been doing real work in tumor boards. Bertsimas and colleagues went back into the original RCT data with an interpretable AI method and found a liposarcoma subset where the benefit is real and was being averaged to null. The question this week is what we owe that finding — and what we owe negative trials before we file them.
JUNO [dry]: The answer is not "nothing."
CASPAR [grounded]: No. But it's not "change the protocol" either. Let's work through it.
CASPAR [carrying]: From Lancet Digital Health, 2026: Bertsimas and colleagues, an interpretable AI reanalysis of the STRASS randomized trial — identifying which retroperitoneal sarcoma patients actually benefit from preoperative radiotherapy.
JUNO [direct]: STRASS first. What was it actually designed to answer?
CASPAR [precise]: Primary retroperitoneal sarcoma — predominantly two histologies, liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma. Patients randomized to preoperative external-beam radiotherapy followed by surgery, or surgery alone. The hypothesis was that neoadjuvant RT would reduce local recurrence in tumors that can't always be resected with wide margins. Primary endpoint was abdominal recurrence-free survival. Overall: no significant difference across the full population.
JUNO [pressing]: If histologic heterogeneity was known going in — and it was, liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma respond to radiation differently — why wasn't the primary analysis stratified by histology?
CASPAR [measured]: It was pre-specified as a subgroup. But subgroup analyses in negative trials have a credibility problem. You're slicing after the fact, you inherit a multiple-comparisons concern, and the field has learned to discount them. The Bertsimas method is a direct response to that.
JUNO [pulling]: So what do optimal policy trees actually do differently?
CASPAR [methodical]: Standard post-hoc subgroup analysis: you pick a cut after seeing the data — histology, tumor size, whatever — run the comparison in that slice, report the p-value. The problem is real even with pre-specification, because the pre-specification list is usually long. Optimal policy trees search systematically for subgroups with heterogeneous treatment effects while preserving the integrity of the original randomization as the source of causal inference. The randomized assignment still identifies the effect — the algorithm is finding where that effect is concentrated, not manufacturing a new comparison. And the output is human-readable decision rules. A clinician can look at the tree and follow the logic. Not a black box.
JUNO [confirming]: So the randomization is intact. The subgroup is discovered, not pre-specified.
CASPAR [holding the line]: Exactly. Sound for hypothesis generation. Cannot substitute for a prospective trial. That ceiling holds.
JUNO [grounded]: What did the trees find?
CASPAR [delivering]: Liposarcoma patients — specifically well-differentiated or dedifferentiated histology, with particular tumor size and grade characteristics — showed a meaningful survival benefit from preoperative RT. That benefit was being diluted to null in the overall analysis by leiomyosarcoma and other histologies where RT showed no advantage. The ITT result was a population-level average.
JUNO [noting]: Liposarcoma is a substantial proportion of retroperitoneal sarcomas in most series. Not a rare edge case.
CASPAR [confirming]: Which is why the dilution matters. If the benefit is real and prospectively confirmed, a substantial proportion of these patients may have been undertreated on the basis of a trial result that was never actually about them.
JUNO [pressing]: What's the magnitude of the benefit in the identified subgroup?
CASPAR [holding]: The direction is clear. I'm not going to cite a specific survival figure from a post-hoc discovery as if it were a prospective estimate. That number needs to come from a trial designed to test this hypothesis.
JUNO [accepting, briefly]: Okay. What changes now?
CASPAR [specific]: Two things. First, how we read the STRASS citation. Citing it as evidence against neoadjuvant RT in retroperitoneal sarcoma without histologic qualification is no longer defensible — it's been doing more work than the data warranted. Second, trial eligibility conversations. Liposarcoma patients who might have been steered away from future RT trials on the basis of STRASS now have a stronger argument for enrollment. What does not change: the treatment decision in the tumor board, absent prospective validation.
JUNO [pushing]: Will the field actually design the confirmatory trial? There's an existing negative result in the literature. That's a real funding headwind.
CASPAR [dry]: The Bertsimas paper is the argument for funding it. Whether that argument lands in a grant review is not a question this paper answers.
