The answer is yes, and the predictor is the same FMF risk tier we used to put them on aspirin in the first place.

CASPAR [grounded]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Lin and colleagues, a secondary analysis of a stepped-wedge cluster RCT across Asian centers — determinants of preterm preeclampsia in aspirin-treated high-risk women.
JUNO [orienting]: The parent trial implemented first-trimester FMF-based screening across multiple centers. Screen-positive women received aspirin. Lin's analysis is restricted to that treated arm — not whether the drug helps, but which of the women who received it still broke through.
CASPAR [methodical]: Secondary analysis of an RCT sits above a retrospective cohort. The constraint is that the parent trial was powered for its primary outcome, not for this subgroup question. That matters before you lean on a nominally significant predictor.
CASPAR [specific]: What they found: first-trimester FMF risk strata and specific biomarkers remained independent predictors of breakthrough preterm preeclampsia in the aspirin-treated arm. The same composite risk score and biomarker burden that flagged these women as high-risk at booking retained prognostic weight after prophylaxis started.
JUNO [flat]: So the risk gradient doesn't compress when you start the drug.
CASPAR [confirming]: Aspirin attenuates risk across the distribution. It does not flatten it. A woman who entered the trial in the highest FMF risk tier remained at higher absolute risk of breakthrough disease than a lower-tier woman on the same drug at the same dose.
JUNO [pressing]: That has a direct implication for how intensively we follow these women.
CASPAR [holding]: It does — and I want to be precise about where the implication lands. This is a secondary analysis from an Asian cohort. Whether FMF strata perform identically as post-aspirin prognostic markers in European or North American populations is a question this paper cannot answer. And the subgroup comparisons were not the pre-specified primary endpoint. A nominally significant predictor in that context is hypothesis-generating.
JUNO [accepting]: So what actually changes?
CASPAR [drawing the line]: The counseling sentence. A woman in the highest FMF risk tier on aspirin should hear that her baseline tier still carries weight — that the drug reduces her risk but does not equalize it with a lower-tier woman. The surveillance protocol does not change, because this paper does not define one. The trial that prospectively tests intensified follow-up for the highest-tier aspirin-treated women hasn't been run. Lin is the argument for running it.
JUNO [dry]: Meaningful, even if it's not the thing you hand the patient today.
CASPAR [brief]: Right.

CASPAR [formal]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Zeng and colleagues, a systematic review and meta-analysis — adverse outcomes in cesarean scar pregnancies continuing beyond the first trimester. Pooled rates of placenta accreta spectrum and hysterectomy in CSP managed expectantly past 14 weeks are very high. CSP subtype and residual myometrial thickness are the key risk modifiers.
JUNO [caveat]: Heterogeneity across included studies is real — outcome definitions, gestational age at diagnosis, intervention thresholds all varied. The direction is unambiguous regardless.
CASPAR [operational]: Document CSP subtype and myometrial thickness at the first imaging encounter. Those are the variables that stratify catastrophic-outcome risk, and if you're having the expectant management conversation, Zeng gives you a pooled denominator to anchor it.
JUNO [formal]: From the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Ben Saleh and colleagues, a nationwide Swedish family study of over 856,000 primiparae — postpartum venous thrombosis and hereditary risk. Graded family history of VTE confers incrementally higher postpartum VTE risk. One affected first-degree relative raises it; multiple affected relatives raise it further. Dose-responsive.
CASPAR [labeling]: Registry data from a relatively homogeneous Swedish population — unmeasured confounders are real, and the demographic homogeneity limits generalizability. Not a trial result.
JUNO [operational]: The clinical hook is intake workflow. For a primipara with two or three affected first-degree relatives and no personal event, this adds quantitative weight to the prophylaxis conversation — but only if hereditary family history is being collected systematically at booking, which in most units it isn't.
CASPAR [formal]: From AJOG MFM, 2026: Benuzzi and colleagues, a population-based cohort of more than 302,000 term pregnancies in Northern Italy — machine learning limits for stillbirth prediction. Advanced ML on registry-level administrative variables achieved only modest AUC for term stillbirth. With 302,000 pregnancies, this is not a power problem.
JUNO [naming it]: It's a signal problem. The variables we routinely collect don't carry enough biological information to get there, regardless of the modeling.
CASPAR [confirming]: Ceiling-effect finding, not a method failure. The method worked — it found the ceiling. What would have to be added to break through it — placental biomarkers, third-trimester imaging, genomics — none of that was in this dataset. That's the study this result is calling for, and it's considerably more expensive to run than a registry analysis.
JUNO [formal]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Cooper and colleagues, a systematic review — diagnostic imaging of adnexal lesions in pregnancy. IOTA-based models applied to adnexal lesions in pregnancy showed variable performance across studies. Heterogeneity was sufficient to preclude formal meta-analysis. No validated pregnancy-specific algorithm exists.
CASPAR [naming the finding]: The inability to meta-analyze is the finding. It reflects how heterogeneous the study populations, lesion types, and outcome definitions were across included studies.
JUNO [clinical]: For now, the honest answer to a referring colleague asking about IOTA in pregnancy is that the non-pregnant validation doesn't transfer cleanly. Expert assessment and MRI for indeterminate cases remain the defensible pathway.
CASPAR [closing]: Two things still open. First: should aspirin-treated women in the highest FMF risk tier receive a formally intensified surveillance protocol, and if so, what does it look like? Lin gives us the predictor. The protocol is not in this paper. Second: what inputs would need to be added to a registry-level dataset to break through the ceiling Benuzzi documents? The 302,000-pregnancy result defines the floor for administrative-variable models. What sits above it is uncharacterized.
JUNO [closing]: The first is a trial design problem. The second is a data collection problem before it's an analysis problem — and that's a harder ask.
