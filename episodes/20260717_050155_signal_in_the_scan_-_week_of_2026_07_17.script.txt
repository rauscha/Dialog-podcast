CASPAR [carrying]: From NPJ Digital Medicine, 2026: Chen and colleagues, a multi-omics machine-learning study in ovarian cancer — predicting chemotherapy response with a random-forest fusion model. The clinical question: platinum-based chemotherapy is first-line, response is heterogeneous, and we have nothing reliable for predicting non-response before the first cycle. If you could identify those patients upfront, you'd have a basis for alternative regimen selection or trial enrollment rather than waiting for failure.
JUNO [pressing]: What makes this different from running three separate analyses and comparing results?
CASPAR [precise]: The integration step. They're not analyzing transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data in parallel — they're fusing all three streams into a single random-forest classifier. Random forests handle high-dimensional, heterogeneous input without the collinearity problem that would collapse a single logistic model. The fusion architecture is what earns the performance claim. Pull that out and the AUC doesn't mean the same thing.
JUNO [holding the frame]: And "before treatment" is the load — that's what makes the complexity worth it.
CASPAR [agreeing]: A post-treatment predictor tells you what you already know. The entire clinical value is in the pre-treatment window.

JUNO [not softening it]: That's a striking result for a small retrospective study.
CASPAR [holding]: Which is precisely why it's hypothesis-generating rather than deployable. CASPAR [carrying]: From Radiology, 2026: Taib and colleagues, a multireader eye-tracking study of screening mammography — automation bias when AI prompts are wrong. Design: radiologists reading screening mammograms with and without AI prompts in a controlled multireader setting, eye-tracking cameras running throughout. The AI is sometimes wrong — false negatives and false positives both. The question is what happens to the reader when it is.
JUNO [naming it]: And the answer isn't just accuracy drops.
CASPAR [precise]: No. When AI flagged a cancer as negative, readers missed it. When AI over-called a lesion, readers over-called it too. The mechanism is gaze-level — incorrect prompts redirected where readers looked, not just what they concluded. That's what the eye-tracking adds. You can see the error propagating before the reader reaches a decision.
JUNO [holding it]: A reader missing a cancer because the AI missed it first is a different failure mode than missing it alone. The error has a traceable source, and that source is inside the workflow.
CASPAR [caveat, no softening]: Controlled multireader setting. Real-world magnitude may differ. Evidence score 0.4. But the behavioral mechanism is demonstrated, not inferred — and for anyone who has already integrated AI into a breast imaging workflow, the question this paper opens is whether mitigation is a training problem, a workflow-design problem, or both. It doesn't answer that.
JUNO [dry]: Which means it's sitting unresolved in every breast imaging department that integrated AI last year.
