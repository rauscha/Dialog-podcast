JUNO [carrying it]: Clara Fernández-Vara, a game studies scholar at NYU, argues that a puzzle is a closed system containing enough information to reach one correct solution through reasoning.
CASPAR [surrogate]: Unpack "closed system."
JUNO [precise]: Everything the player needs is inside the puzzle. The designer isn't allowed to hide a piece. If you need information to solve it, that information has to be there. Fernández-Vara calls it a contract — the designer provides everything required, the player does the reasoning.
CASPAR [pressing]: So what's outside the contract?
JUNO [listing]: Guessing. Luck. Reflex challenges. And what designers call moon logic — solutions that require leaps no reasonable player would make. The name comes from a puzzle in Monkey Island where you inflate a balloon using a pump, but the pump is a dog, and the dog is inflated by a cat, and — it doesn't matter. The solution is technically present in the game world but the reasoning path is severed. You can't get there from here.
CASPAR [flat]: You arrive at the answer by accident or by looking it up.
JUNO [exactly]: Which means when you finally "solve" it, you don't feel clever. You feel relieved. The sudden collapse of confusion into clarity. He separates that sharply from mere difficulty finally worn down.
CASPAR [taking it]: Players don't feel like they're signing a contract. They feel like they're stuck.
JUNO [small laugh]: Right. And then something happens. The contract is what makes that something feel earned rather than arbitrary. When it breaks — when the designer withheld something, or the solution required a leap — you don't get satisfaction. You get relief that it's over. Those are different feelings. The second one is what you're trying to build.
CASPAR [pivoting]: Picture a player in a chair. Alone. Staring at a screen. The puzzle hasn't changed. They have. Something reorganizes — not in the game, in their head — and their hand moves. That's insight. The psychological term for the sudden mental reorganization when a solution that was invisible becomes obvious. Studied in cognitive science since the 1920s, when Gestalt psychologists noticed that solutions didn't arrive gradually — they arrived all at once.
JUNO [curious]: Is that actually distinct from just — figuring something out slowly?
CASPAR [yes]: Grant draws the line sharply. Difficulty from obscurity produces relief. You were missing a piece, you found it, you're glad it's over. Insight produces satisfaction — you feel like you did something real. The model in your head snapped into a new configuration. Different event.
JUNO [following]: So the designer's job isn't to make something hard.
CASPAR [exactly]: The designer's job is to manufacture a specific event inside a stranger's brain. Hard is not the target. Insight is the target.
JUNO [friction]: How does a designer tell the difference from the inside? Between a puzzle that produces insight and one that just produces relief?
CASPAR [thinking]: Mostly they can't. Which is — that's actually why there's a structural problem baked into this craft that we need to name before anything else.
JUNO [carrying]: A designer has just solved their own puzzle for the hundredth time. They're trying to remember what it felt like not to know the answer. And they can't. Patrick Traynor — designer of Patrick's Parabox, a 2022 puzzle game about boxes inside boxes — names this directly. Developers become extremely good at their own puzzles. Which blinds them to actual difficulty. Even a level they think is trivial can be genuinely hard for someone who hasn't been living inside the system for months.
CASPAR [surrogate]: Is that just a matter of playtesting more?
JUNO [no]: It's deeper than that. There's a documented cognitive bias called the curse of knowledge — knowing something makes it nearly impossible to imagine not knowing it. You can't un-know the solution. Every judgment you make about whether a puzzle is too hard, too easy, teaches the right thing — all of it is contaminated by knowledge the player doesn't have. This isn't a personality flaw. It's structural.
CASPAR [extending]: And this is the reason every other technique in this episode exists. You are the worst possible judge of your own puzzle. Not because you're careless. Because you cannot access the cognitive state your player is in. Puzzle design is permanently empirical — you cannot think your way out of the curse. You have to watch someone else play.
JUNO [landing it]: Keep that in mind for everything that follows. Every step in the method is a workaround for a problem you can never fully solve.
CASPAR [pivoting]: Before we get to the workarounds, there's a real disagreement among the best designers about what you're even trying to build.
JUNO [surrogate]: What's the actual disagreement?
CASPAR [laying it out]: Traynor's position: make puzzles as simple as possible while still demonstrating the idea. The system is the star. Trim possibility space. Remove solution steps. Keep things bite-sized. His game is built around pushing boxes that contain other boxes, including the box you're standing in — that mechanic is genuinely mind-bending — and his philosophy is to find the simplest puzzle that shows you something true about that mechanic, then move to the next thing. Each individual puzzle is a demonstration of the underlying rule-set.
JUNO [following]: And Blow?
CASPAR [measured]: Blow says difficulty should come from the system's genuine complexity, not from simplifying it away. He's on record saying he'll "get rid of fun" if it means getting at something deeper or more complete. The goal isn't maximizing moment-to-moment enjoyment. It's insight — and sometimes insight requires wrestling with something that doesn't yield easily.
JUNO [pushing]: Is that actually a disagreement, or are they just describing different games?
CASPAR [direct]: It's a real disagreement about what the player's satisfaction should come from. Feeling clever because the system revealed itself — that's Traynor's target. Feeling clever because you wrestled something genuinely hard to the ground — that's Blow's. Both are valid. They produce different games. The listener's job is to know which one they're building, because the choice shapes every downstream decision.
JUNO [taking it]: So. You've picked a philosophy. Now you need actual puzzles. And there's a specific process for generating them.
CASPAR [surrogate]: Is this where the Witness line comes back?
JUNO [yes]: Exactly here. Blow, months into The Witness, drawing a new line on a panel. The moment constraint discovery stops being an accident and becomes a method. Start with a core interaction — the single, fundamental thing the player does, the action everything else is built around — and then ask "what if" questions until new mechanics emerge from the system itself. What if the line can't cross itself? What if it has to pass through every region? What if certain cells are forbidden? The constraint isn't imposed from outside. It's found inside the rules that already exist.
CASPAR [surrogate]: How do you know when you've found a good constraint versus a dead end?
JUNO [landing the callback]: Earlier I said he found the rule, he didn't invent it. You deferred the question. You can actually answer it now.
CASPAR [answering]: You know you've found a good constraint when it generates puzzles you didn't anticipate. When the system surprises you. That's the signal. JUNO [follow-up]: What if you never get surprised?
CASPAR [clean]: Then you probably haven't found your core interaction yet. Go back one step. The constraint discovery process only works if the core interaction is genuinely generative. If it isn't, no amount of "what if" questions will save it.
JUNO [transitioning]: You have puzzles. Now you have to put them in order. And the order is doing more work than it looks like.
CASPAR [carrying]: The sequence determines what the player knows when they encounter each puzzle. Get it wrong and the aha never arrives — the player either breezes through because they already know everything, or hits a wall because they don't know enough yet. Sequencing is the curriculum.
JUNO [surrogate]: What does "curriculum" mean in practice?
CASPAR [teaching/testing]: There's a distinction designers use: teaching versus testing. A teaching puzzle introduces a mechanic in a low-stakes context — no time pressure, no penalty for failure, the solution space is constrained enough that the player almost has to discover the rule. A testing puzzle asks the player to apply that mechanic under pressure, in combination with other things they know. You need both. You can't skip the teaching step and go straight to testing. The player won't know what they're being tested on.
JUNO [concrete]: Portal. Valve's 2007 puzzle game where you shoot linked portals into walls to move through space.
CASPAR [yes]: Test Chamber 01 didn't exist in the original design. Playtesting revealed players were reaching level two without understanding how portals worked. The lesson is delivered through movement, not explanation. Most comprehension problems disappeared.
JUNO [pressing]: There's a heuristic I've seen — don't introduce a mechanic more than five levels before the player needs it.
CASPAR [careful]: That's Traynor's five-level heuristic, and I want to be precise: it's a useful starting point, not a law. If a player won't use a mechanic for five levels, they'll forget it.
