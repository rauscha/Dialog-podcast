Three European hospitals, prospective enrollment, expert consensus ground truth. The answer was yes. With a ceiling worth knowing before anyone books a procurement meeting.
CASPAR [grounded]: From Radiology, 2026: Ruitenbeek and colleagues, a multinational multicenter validation across three European hospitals — external testing of an AI tool for appendicular fracture detection on plain radiographs.
JUNO [carrying the design]: The clinical context is emergency and outpatient radiology — plain radiograph reads for suspected wrist, ankle, shoulder fractures. AI as second reader. Prospective enrollment, ground truth from expert consensus plus follow-up imaging where the initial read was uncertain. The sites didn't build the tool.
CASPAR [precise]: That's what earns the evidence grade. Geographically distinct centers, standardized digital radiography, adult populations. Diagnostic accuracy held across all three sites. When clinicians used the AI as a second reader, sensitivity and specificity both improved — across fracture types, across anatomic locations. Not one body part, not one fracture pattern.
JUNO [noting]: Consistency across sites is the result. A tool that lifts sensitivity at one center and degrades specificity at another isn't deployable.
CASPAR [confirming]: Ruitenbeek shows neither degradation.
JUNO [turning]: Now the ceiling.
CASPAR [naming it]: All three sites were European academic centers with standardized digital radiography throughout. Nothing on lower-resource settings, older CR equipment, pediatric trauma volumes, non-European fracture epidemiology. The study geography is the limit — that's not a design flaw, the study was honest about its population.
JUNO [pressing]: So what does a center outside that profile actually have?
CASPAR [dry]: A strong prior and an untested assumption. Local validation before go-live is still the only way to know whether this result applies to a specific deployment.

JUNO [moving]: From Radiology, 2026: Coelho and colleagues, a large single-center retrospective study — a diagnostically calibrated AI framework for prostate MRI image quality assessment. The standard approach is a reader's subjective label — adequate, suboptimal, non-diagnostic. Coelho derives quality scores algorithmically, calibrated to what actually predicts downstream AI detection accuracy for clinically significant prostate cancer. In their dataset, those scores outperform the reader labels in predicting how the AI performs on a given scan.
CASPAR [holding the caveat]: Single-center, retrospective — external validation is the next step before this touches pipeline design. But if your quality gate is currently based on reader labels, this paper gives you a specific reason to ask whether those labels predict what you think they predict.
JUNO [next]: From Radiology, 2026: Zhao and colleagues, a prospective cohort with a novel PET tracer — 68Ga-NK224 imaging of whole-body PD-L1 heterogeneity in non-small cell lung cancer. The tracer targets PD-L1 directly. Whole-body imaging showed substantial expression variation between lesions in the same patient, and between regions within a single lesion. Lesion-specific uptake correlated with checkpoint inhibitor response. The comparison with single-biopsy IHC favored the PET approach, though the cohort is moderate in size and single-center.
CASPAR [flat]: The single-biopsy problem in PD-L1 assessment has been named for years. This is prospective whole-body data that begins to quantify how much expression you're missing from one tissue sample. The trial that acts on it hasn't been designed yet.
