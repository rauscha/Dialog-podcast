JUNO [pressing]: And the finding?
CASPAR [measured]: Women who started before 12 weeks had significantly lower rates of the composite endpoint. The difference was clinically meaningful, not marginal.
JUNO [testing]: So the timing moves the needle in exactly the group where we've been loosest about it.
CASPAR [immediate]: That's what the data suggest. And I want to name the confound before you ask. Women presenting before 12 weeks are not a random draw from this population. They may be more health-engaged, face fewer access barriers, have more prenatal planning. Early aspirin and early presentation are structurally entangled in a retrospective design. You can't fully separate the timing effect from the presentation-timing effect.
JUNO [pressing]: Is the confound large enough to explain the whole result?
CASPAR [considered]: Possibly not entirely — but this is single-center, not a linked national registry, so the bounds on that confound are harder to estimate than they'd be with broader population data. The confound is real. It's not obviously sufficient to account for the full magnitude of the difference. Prospective confirmation isn't optional here — it's the required next step.

CASPAR [carrying]: From AJOG, 2026: Nachum and colleagues, a sibling-controlled cohort study — in utero GDM exposure and long-term metabolic and ovarian outcomes in daughters. Daughters born during a GDM-affected pregnancy had higher rates of PCOS and metabolic syndrome compared to their own sisters born from non-GDM pregnancies. The sibling-controlled design handles shared familial and genetic background — that's what earns this paper more than the standard observational caveat.
JUNO [quick]: The intrauterine environment still varies between pregnancies, even between siblings.
CASPAR [agreed]: It does. The design controls for shared genes and household, not for every pregnancy-specific exposure. Whether this changes surveillance protocols for daughters is downstream of this paper. The directional counseling implication is real.
