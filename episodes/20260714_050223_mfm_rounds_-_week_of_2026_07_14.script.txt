JUNO [orienting]: The clinical scenario is a patient presenting with advanced cervical dilation, membranes at or near the os. CASPAR [precise]: The design question is whether you can run a proper RCT in that setting at all. Non-blinded by necessity — you can't sham a surgical technique in an acute presentation. Both arms received vaginal progesterone as background therapy. That's the realistic ceiling for this question, and it's the first time anyone's cleared it.
JUNO [pressing]: So what does non-blinding actually cost you here?
CASPAR [working it out]: Performance bias is real — surgeons who know which arm they're in may manage the subsequent pregnancy differently. Ascertainment bias if outcome assessors aren't masked. Neither is trivial. But the alternative is no randomized data, and we've had that for decades. The design earns its grade for what it is.
JUNO [dry]: Right. So what do the numbers say.
CASPAR [delivering]: Primary outcomes were pregnancy prolongation and neonatal survival. Double-level cerclage showed improvement in both. The effect is meaningful enough to prompt a protocol conversation. It is not large enough to be read as definitive across all settings.

CASPAR [carrying]: From BJOG, 2026: Adu-Bredu and colleagues, a methodological analysis — internal consistency of the Hadlock estimated fetal weight reference.
JUNO [grounding]: The Hadlock EFW reference — published in the mid-1980s, derived from a US population, still the most widely embedded reference in ultrasound software globally. Adu-Bredu and colleagues compared the published centile chart to its underlying regression equation and found systematic discrepancies concentrated near the 3rd and 10th centiles.
CASPAR [precise]: Which are exactly the thresholds where clinical decisions turn. Enhanced surveillance, delivery timing, NICU notification — all of those hinge on whether a fetus is below the 10th or the 3rd.
