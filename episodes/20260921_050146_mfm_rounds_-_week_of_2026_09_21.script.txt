CASPAR [carrying]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Yerlikaya-Schatten and colleagues, a multicenter cohort — preeclampsia incidence in triplet pregnancies with and without embryo reduction.
JUNO [finding]: Preeclampsia in non-reduced triplets: approximately 10%. Triplets reduced to singleton: approximately 2%. Triplets reduced to twins: also approximately 10% — statistically indistinguishable from the non-reduced group.
CASPAR [caveat]: Selection bias is immediate. Patients who reduce to singleton may differ systematically from those who stop at twins — different risk profiles, different counseling access. These groups aren't randomized.
JUNO [clinical hook]: Still — if a patient is asking whether reducing to twins is sufficient to meaningfully lower her preeclampsia risk, this data says it may not be. CASPAR [carrying]: From BJOG, 2026: Rooyen and colleagues, a secondary analysis of two randomized trials — proteinuria thresholds and outcomes in expectantly managed preterm preeclampsia.
JUNO [finding]: Proteinuria at or above 5 grams per 24 hours: maternal complication rate 28%, latency roughly 5 days shorter than lower-proteinuria cases. Below 3 grams: complication rate 9%.
CASPAR [caveat]: Secondary analyses of trials not designed or powered to test proteinuria as a stratification variable. The subgroup estimates carry wider uncertainty than the numbers alone suggest. These are not validated decision-rule cutoffs.
JUNO [clinical hook]: The 28% versus 9% gap is large enough that it belongs in the shared decision-making conversation during expectant management. Use it in the counseling room. Whether it belongs in a written protocol algorithm needs a prospective answer.
CASPAR [carrying]: From AJOG, 2026: Bart and colleagues, a single-center retrospective study — antihypertensive escalation during expectant management of early-onset severe preeclampsia.
JUNO [finding and caveat]: Escalating antihypertensive therapy was associated with worse maternal outcomes. Single-center retrospective, almost certainly a severity marker — patients requiring more medication are sicker. The medications aren't causing the worse outcomes.
CASPAR [clinical hook]: Treat escalation as a reassessment trigger. If you're adding a second or third agent and pressures still aren't controlled, that's a signal to revisit whether expectant management should continue — not a signal that the drugs are harmful.
JUNO [carrying]: From AJOG MFM, 2026: Bladfält and colleagues, a population-based prospective cohort — maternal BMI and oxytocin requirements during labor induction.
CASPAR [finding]: Higher maternal BMI independently associated with greater cumulative oxytocin dose and higher maximum infusion rates during induction in nulliparous women. The biological rationale — altered volume of distribution, modified receptor sensitivity — predates this paper.
JUNO [caveat]: Demonstrating the association is not the same as showing that adjusting the protocol improves delivery outcomes. Normalizing pharmacokinetics and improving cesarean rates are different claims, and this paper only makes the first one.
CASPAR [clinical hook]: For units still running fixed-dose protocols, this is the paper that makes deferring a BMI-adjusted approach harder to defend. The association is now prospective and population-based.
