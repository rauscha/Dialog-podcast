CASPAR [flat, direct]: Closed spinal dysraphism has a counseling problem. Not a diagnosis problem — we've been identifying it prenatally for years. The problem is what comes after: a family asks what their child's motor function, bladder, and bowel will look like at 18 months, 36 months, and the honest answer has been "we think it depends on the subtype, but the outcome data isn't really there for CSD specifically."
JUNO [even]: That sentence has carried a lot of weight in a lot of difficult rooms.
CASPAR [same register]: It's no longer accurate. From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Athiel and colleagues, a 10-year two-center cohort of 70 fetuses — prenatal ultrasound features and postnatal functional outcomes in closed spinal dysraphism.
JUNO [carries it forward]: Two centers, a decade of follow-up, systematic prenatal characterization across CSD subtypes — lipomyelomeningocele, lipomyelocele, terminal myelocystocele, and others — linked to standardized postnatal functional assessment at 18 and 36 months.
CASPAR [on design]: The retrospective structure is a limitation, and imaging protocols evolved over the decade — those two facts travel together. What earns the evidence grade is the combination of systematic prenatal characterization and standardized postnatal assessment. Without both, you can't make the prenatal-to-outcome link. This cohort makes it.
JUNO [on clinical framing]: The family sitting across from you isn't asking about CSD in general. They're asking about their lesion, their level, whether their child will walk independently, whether they'll have bladder control. That's the question this design is built to answer.
CASPAR [on findings]: What the cohort delivers is a mapping of specific prenatal imaging phenotypes to functional trajectories by subtype. Certain lesion types and locations correlate with better or worse motor and sphincter outcomes at 36 months. For lipomyelomeningocele versus terminal myelocystocele, the trajectories are different enough that the counseling conversation should be different. Before this, you were either extrapolating from open NTD literature or citing small retrospective series with limited follow-up.
JUNO [presses]: How confident can we actually be at the subtype level?
CASPAR [holds]: N equals 70 is the largest CSD prenatal-to-outcome cohort we have. Within-subtype numbers for the rarer lesion types will be small. The directional findings are informative. Subtype-level estimates are not definitive. That distinction matters when you're citing this in a counseling room.
JUNO [slight revision]: I came into this thinking "directional" was a fairly weak concession. But for a condition where we've been counseling from borrowed evidence, directional-and-specific-to-CSD is a meaningful step up.

CASPAR [precise]: The 36-month ceiling is not a footnote. It's the boundary of what you can cite.
JUNO [flat]: "Here's what the cohort shows for your specific lesion type" is a different sentence than "we think it depends on the subtype." That's real.
CASPAR [on practice change]: What changes Monday: subtype-specific prognostication is now evidence-grounded rather than extrapolated. The conversation can be more granular — lesion type, location, associated posterior fossa findings — rather than categorical. JUNO [first round]: From Prenatal Diagnosis, 2026: Lu and colleagues, a cohort study of cfDNA screening after PGT-A euploid embryo transfer — concordance with amniocentesis and residual aneuploidy risk. The clinical assumption in some centers is that a confirmed euploid transfer makes cfDNA in the pregnancy redundant, or at minimum more reliable. Lu tests that directly.
CASPAR [finding]: cfDNA PPV for aneuploidy after a euploid transfer was 12.5% in this cohort. Residual aneuploidy risk was 0.1%. Chromosomal microarray at amniocentesis added 0.5% clinically relevant submicroscopic findings not captured by cfDNA or standard karyotype.
JUNO [caveat]: Single large cohort; PGT-A practice patterns vary. The 12.5% PPV is a counseling number for this population, not a reason to abandon cfDNA post-PGT-A.
CASPAR [clinical hook]: The number worth pausing on is the CMA yield — 0.5% survives even when cfDNA adds little. If you're counseling about invasive testing after a euploid transfer, CMA at amniocentesis has an independent argument. And the 12.5% PPV needs to be spoken out loud — a lot of clinicians are implicitly assuming cfDNA is more reliable in this population, not less.
JUNO [second round]: From Prenatal Diagnosis, 2026: Shinar and colleagues, a comprehensive narrative review — fetal intracranial hemorrhage etiology, workup, and counseling framework. Fetal ICH is rare, heterogeneous in etiology, and the literature is correspondingly scattered.
CASPAR [evidence grade, immediate]: Narrative review. The outcome numbers Shinar cites come from the underlying studies, not from this paper. Label it as a counseling framework, not a primary data source. The underlying literature for fetal ICH is heterogeneous and relatively thin.
JUNO [clinical hook]: The framework value is real even so. When fetal ICH lands unexpectedly on an anatomy scan, you need a structure for the workup conversation and the prognosis conversation. CASPAR [third round]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Chonnak and colleagues, a multicenter external validation study — sagittal aortic-arch assessment methods for prenatal coarctation diagnosis. Ten published sagittal aortic-arch echocardiographic parameters externally validated in a multicenter cohort, with a new multivariable prediction model introduced alongside.
JUNO [finding and caveat]: The multicenter design strengthens generalizability relative to single-center validation. Even the best-performing model has imperfect discrimination for CoA prenatally — local expertise and operator experience remain part of the result.
CASPAR [clinical hook]: If you're currently using a single parameter, this paper tells you which ones hold up in external validation. If your center is considering a model-based approach, this is the current benchmark. Whether the model outperforms simpler approaches in routine practice is a separate question this paper doesn't answer.
JUNO [fourth round]: From Fetal Diagnosis and Therapy, 2026: Bhatia and colleagues, a longitudinal cohort — isolated narrow cavum septi pellucidi from prenatal diagnosis through early childhood neurodevelopment. Narrow CSP is a relatively common incidental finding on second-trimester imaging; the question is how often it resolves and what the neurodevelopmental picture looks like when it does.
CASPAR [numbers]: Fifty-eight percent normalized by late second trimester. Among infants followed to 2 years, 81.5% had normal neurodevelopment. Largest longitudinal cohort with neurodevelopmental follow-up for this finding.
JUNO [caveat]: "Isolated" is carrying significant weight here. CASPAR [clinical hook]: For a genuinely isolated narrow CSP with no associated findings, this cohort supports a reassurance conversation with continued surveillance. The 18.5% who did not have normal neurodevelopment at 2 years is the number that keeps the surveillance warranted.
