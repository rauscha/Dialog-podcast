This document will be cited in protocol reviews and credentialing discussions for years. The question it answers isn't whether cerclage works. It's which patients, indicated how, at what confidence level.
JUNO [grounded]: And the answer is different depending on which of the three categories you're in. That's the structure worth holding onto.
CASPAR [measured]: Right. This is a consolidation event. The value is in the grading, not the conclusions.
CASPAR [delivering]: From Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2026: Thagard, Shields, Gyamfi-Bannerman, and the ACOG Committee on Clinical Practice Guidelines — an evidence-based practice guideline consolidating indications, techniques, and management for cervical cerclage. Modified GRADE means it synthesizes RCT and cohort data and produces explicit evidence grades — not just recommendations. The grades differ by indication, and the document says so openly.
JUNO [naming the structure]: Three categories. History-indicated — prior spontaneous preterm birth or second-trimester loss, current pregnancy with a short cervix or prior cerclage. Ultrasound-indicated — short cervix found on mid-trimester scan in a singleton with a prior preterm birth. And physical-exam-indicated: rescue cerclage, placed when the cervix is already dilated, membranes possibly visible.
CASPAR [on the grades]: History-indicated has the strongest evidence base. RCT data support a meaningful reduction in preterm birth in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth and a cervical length below 25 millimeters in the current pregnancy. Ultrasound-indicated in singletons with prior preterm birth and a short cervix also carries favorable evidence — RCT-level — and the guideline is explicit that cerclage is preferred over progesterone alone in that population.
JUNO [pressing]: And rescue?
CASPAR [direct]: Cohort data only. The populations across studies are heterogeneous, the indication isn't consistently defined across centers, and the guideline names that directly. It doesn't recommend against rescue cerclage — it grades the evidence as weaker and says the decision involves clinical judgment in a way the other two categories don't. That's the most honest part of the document. It doesn't manufacture confidence where the data don't support it.
JUNO [noting]: Which is also where most of the practice variation lives.
CASPAR [on technique]: On McDonald versus Shirodkar — head-to-head RCT data are sparse. The guideline doesn't pick one. Both are acceptable; technique choice reflects operator experience and anatomical considerations. That's an accurate description of what the trial literature actually supports.
JUNO [pressing on practice]: So what changes? For a center doing this well?
CASPAR [honest]: For centers consistent with the evidence — it confirms. The counseling language gets more precise. For a patient with prior spontaneous preterm birth and a short cervix on mid-trimester scan, you now have an explicit evidence grade to cite when explaining why cerclage is preferred over progesterone alone. That's a different conversation than "this is what we do here." For centers where practice has drifted — cerclage in twins without prior preterm birth, or defaulting to progesterone in populations where cerclage has RCT support — this is the document that prompts the protocol review.
JUNO [identifying the signal]: The multiple gestation line is probably the most operationally consequential sentence in the document for centers where that's been variable. Cerclage in multiple gestations without prior preterm birth is not supported.
CASPAR [confirming]: The guideline is explicit on that. Perioperative management recommendations are included but are largely consistent with existing practice at high-volume centers. Nothing procedurally novel there.

CASPAR [delivering]: From Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2026: Theilen and colleagues, a secondary analysis of an RCT — thyroid function trajectories through pregnancy and one year postpartum in subclinical hypothyroidism and hypothyroxinemia. TPO antibody status is the stratifying variable. Women with subclinical hypothyroidism who are TPO-positive are more likely to remain hypothyroid or worsen through pregnancy and into the postpartum year. TPO-negative women show meaningfully higher rates of spontaneous normalization. Hypothyroxinemia follows a distinct trajectory from subclinical hypothyroidism — the two are not interchangeable on this outcome.
JUNO [on the caveat]: Secondary analysis. The parent RCT wasn't powered for all subgroup comparisons by antibody status. The trajectory data are descriptive natural history, not a randomized comparison of monitoring strategies.
CASPAR [adding the hook]: The TPO stratification is consistent with the mechanistic picture — this isn't a surprise finding, it's a quantified one. If you're not checking TPO status at the time of the initial thyroid diagnosis in pregnancy, this paper is a reason to reconsider that practice. The optimal monitoring interval for TPO-negative women who normalize isn't answered here. But the stratification is now grounded in RCT-adjacent data.
JUNO [moving]: From AJOG MFM, 2026: Sebag and colleagues, a population-based analysis of 3.4 million births — continuous spline modeling of pre-pregnancy BMI and obstetric outcomes. When BMI is modeled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines rather than standard categorical cutoffs, the risk relationships are nonlinear and outcome-specific. At the extremes — both high and low BMI — the familiar class boundaries appear to underestimate risk. Gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, and neonatal outcomes each have distinct BMI-risk curves.
CASPAR [on the caveat]: Observational. US birth certificate data, subject to the usual coding and ascertainment limitations. The denominator is large, but the design doesn't establish causality.
JUNO [on the hook]: The counseling implication is concrete. A patient at BMI 42 is not simply "class III obese" with a fixed risk increment over BMI 35. The absolute risk estimates at the extremes are higher than categorical framing suggests. Whether your risk communication tools are built around continuous or categorical BMI is now a question worth asking explicitly — though the continuous model may be too granular to be operationally useful at the bedside for most centers.
CASPAR [dry]: It raises the issue more than it resolves it.
JUNO [acknowledging]: Yeah.
CASPAR [moving]: From Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2026: Litman and colleagues, a block-randomized QI study of about 1,100 patients — comparing three postpartum oxytocin infusion rates and quantitative blood loss after vaginal birth. Higher-rate infusion reduced median quantitative blood loss compared to lower rates.
JUNO [on the caveat]: Block-randomized QI is not a traditional RCT. Sequential blocks mean temporal confounding is possible — staffing changes, concurrent protocol shifts, patient population drift.
CASPAR [on the hook]: Don't cite this as RCT evidence. Cite it as the best available QI data on a question that probably won't get a traditional RCT. If your unit's postpartum oxytocin protocol hasn't been reviewed recently, this paper supports considering a higher default infusion rate. Whether benefit continues to scale above the highest rate tested here is uncharacterized. The direction is consistent with the physiology.
JUNO [last round]: From Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2026: Trawick and colleagues, a retrospective cohort of over 560,000 sequential pregnancies — recurrence risk after severe preeclampsia, HELLP, or eclampsia. Prior severe hypertensive disease of pregnancy confers approximately 15% recurrence risk in a subsequent pregnancy. Earlier gestational age at index delivery is the strongest predictor — women who delivered before 34 weeks carry meaningfully higher recurrence risk than those at or after 34 weeks.
CASPAR [on the caveat]: Retrospective US cohort. Chronic hypertension, renal disease, interpregnancy interval, BMI trajectory — not fully adjustable. The 15% is a population estimate; individual risk varies with the predictors named.
JUNO [on the hook]: Fifteen percent is high enough that it changes the framing of the preconception visit. And the gestational-age gradient means the counseling isn't the same for every patient who had severe disease. Prior work gave directional guidance. This dataset gives you an absolute risk estimate stratified by a clinically available predictor. Whether that risk is modifiable — through interpregnancy weight loss, blood pressure optimization, aspirin timing — is not answered here. But the counseling sentence is more precise than it was before this paper.
CASPAR [closing]: Four open threads. Rescue cerclage: the guideline names the evidence gap accurately, and that gap doesn't close until someone designs a prospective trial with a consistently defined indication. No such trial is currently running. Thyroid monitoring interval: Theilen gives you the TPO stratification but not the optimal recheck schedule. Oxytocin ceiling: Litman's highest rate reduced blood loss; whether a higher rate would continue that trend or plateau is uncharacterized, and oxytocin receptor saturation is relevant and unaddressed in this dataset. Recurrence modifiability: Trawick quantifies the risk. Whether intervening on modifiable factors changes the approximately 15% recurrence rate requires a prospective intervention study.
JUNO [clean]: The cerclage guideline and the Trawick cohort are the most immediately usable at the bedside. The thyroid and oxytocin questions are the ones worth watching for the next trial.
