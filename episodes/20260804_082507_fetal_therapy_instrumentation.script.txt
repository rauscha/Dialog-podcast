JUNO [grounded, setting up]: Two objects on a table. The first is a Harrison shunt — a metal cannula, roughly the diameter of a pencil lead, designed in the 1980s. It's a tube. You push it through the mother's abdomen into the uterus, through the uterine wall, into whatever fluid collection is threatening the fetus, and it drains. That's the whole design. The second is a Somatex shunt, from 2014. Same job. But when you deploy it, two small parasol-shaped flanges spring open on either side of the uterine wall and anchor it in place.
CASPAR [following]: Both for the same condition?
JUNO [precise]: LUTO — Lower Urinary Tract Obstruction. A blockage that stops the fetus's bladder from draining. If you don't treat it before birth, the kidneys fail, the lungs don't develop, and the baby doesn't survive. So you place a shunt — a tiny tube that bypasses the blockage and lets fluid drain into the amniotic sac. That's fetal therapy: surgical intervention on a fetus while it's still in the uterus, treating something that would be fatal if you waited until after birth.
CASPAR [landing on it]: And the two shunts produce different outcomes.
JUNO [flat]: Fifty percent live birth rate with the Harrison. Eighty-four point eight with the Somatex. The surgeons didn't change. The hospitals didn't change. The instrument changed.
CASPAR [quiet]: Thirty-five points.
JUNO [yes]: Which is why the instrument is not a footnote. It's the story. The question that immediately follows — if the instrument is the variable, why did it take until 2014 to change it — that answer requires understanding the regulatory world these devices live in. But first there's something genuinely strange about this field before you even get to the survival numbers.
CASPAR [picking it up]: Every surgical instrument ever made is designed for one patient.
JUNO [completing it]: And fetal surgery instruments are designed for two.
CASPAR [carrying it]: A surgeon at an operating table. A pregnant patient. And a second patient — the fetus — who cannot consent, cannot be examined directly, cannot say where it hurts. Every instrument in that surgeon's hand has to treat both of them simultaneously, without the surgeon ever directly seeing one of them.
JUNO [grounding it]: The two-patient problem — that's actually what people in the field call it. And it's not philosophical. It has a concrete mechanical consequence. Every millimeter of instrument diameter carries a risk profile for two people. The uterus is a muscular organ that contracts when it's disturbed. Too large an instrument and you trigger preterm labor. Too small and you can't do the procedure. Every material choice, every geometry decision — all of it optimized against two patients whose interests are not always aligned.
CASPAR [following]: And the history of the field is basically a history of trying to shrink the access point.
JUNO [yes]: Early fetal surgery used a hysterotomy — an actual surgical incision into the uterus. Effective, but the maternal risk is significant. The field moved toward fetoscopic approaches: a narrow telescope-like instrument, one to three millimeters in diameter, inserted without opening the uterus surgically. Smaller access, less maternal disruption — but now the fetus is harder to see, harder to reach, and the instruments have to do more work through a narrower channel.
CASPAR [dry]: Solve one problem, inherit the next.
JUNO [matching it]: Reasonable summary of the last forty years.
CASPAR [moving]: Before the instruments make sense, we need to know what conditions they actually treat — and which ones have actually been proven to work.
JUNO [taking it]: Two papers. Pin them to the wall. The MOMS trial, 2011. Spina bifida — a birth defect where the spinal column doesn't fully close, leaving spinal cord tissue exposed. MOMS proved that closing that lesion before birth produced better neurological outcomes than waiting until after delivery. First randomized controlled trial to definitively prove a fetal surgery intervention worked. Set the template.
CASPAR [following]: And the second?
JUNO [measured]: The TOTAL trial, 2021. CDH — Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia — a hole in the fetus's diaphragm that lets abdominal organs crowd into the chest, preventing the lungs from developing. The trial tested a procedure called FETO — Fetoscopic Endoluminal Tracheal Occlusion — a small balloon placed in the fetal airway via fetoscope. The balloon blocks airflow, which creates pressure that stimulates lung growth.
CASPAR [anticipating]: And it worked.
JUNO [careful]: For severe cases. Survival to discharge went from approximately fifteen percent to forty percent. For moderate CDH — same procedure, different starting point — no benefit. The trial had to discover that the biology of the intervention window matters in a way the design couldn't anticipate in advance.
CASPAR [flagging]: Which means the procedure that was just proven to work is now being concentrated at fewer centers. Hospitals that were building FETO programs for moderate cases — they're scaling back or stopping. The expertise and the caseload both pull toward the specialist centers that ran the trials. The procedure gets rarer, fewer surgeons do it, the training pipeline tightens.
JUNO [yes]: We'll come back to that. But first — what are we actually working with, instrument-wise?
CASPAR [laying it out]: Four categories. A fetoscope — one to three millimeters in diameter, inserted through the mother's abdomen to visualize the fetus and guide instruments. A laser fiber — a thin optical cable threaded through the fetoscope that delivers laser energy to seal blood vessels, which is called photocoagulation. That's the instrument for TTTS — Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome, where shared placental blood vessels in an identical twin pregnancy cause one twin to receive too much blood and one too little. A tracheal balloon — the FETO device; the specific one used clinically is the BALT Goldballoon, made by a French company called BALT. And the shunt — the Harrison or the Somatex from the top of the episode.
JUNO [noting]: Four categories. Everything else in the field is a variation on one of those.
CASPAR [flipping it]: Four categories sounds like substantial coverage for a field most people have never heard of. Or it's a remarkably thin portfolio for conditions that together affect tens of thousands of pregnancies a year globally. Those aren't the same observation.
JUNO [conceding]: Fair point.
CASPAR [continuing]: And of those four categories, one company holds the regulatory position on the most-used instrument. That story starts in 2006. Karl Storz — a German medical device company — received FDA approval for a fetoscope used in TTTS laser surgery. Nearly twenty years later, it is still the only FDA-approved fetoscope for that indication in the United States.
JUNO [surprised]: Still the only one?
CASPAR [yes]: The approval came through a pathway called the Humanitarian Device Exemption — the HDE. An FDA track for devices treating fewer than 4,000 patients per year in the US at the time of that approval. The bar is lower than a full clinical trial: you show the device is probably beneficial, not that it's superior to existing alternatives. That lower bar got one device approved. It also means any competitor faces the same manufacturing documentation burden, the same sterilization validation, the same failure mode analysis — without being first, and without a market large enough to easily recoup those costs.
JUNO [holding it]: So the pathway that made it possible to approve a device for this few patients also made it nearly impossible for anyone else to enter.
CASPAR [dry]: Feature or bug depends on who you ask.
MAREN [entering, precise]: The HDE documentation burden isn't the barrier people think it is. The barrier is doing it for a market that will never scale the way a hip replacement scales. Sterilization validation alone — proving your device survives the autoclave cycles required between surgical uses without degrading — that's a real cost for a device that might move two thousand units a year globally. The regulatory pathway didn't freeze the field. The economics did. The pathway just made the economics visible.
JUNO [to Maren]: So when we look at the Harrison-to-Somatex transition — what actually changed? Because from the outside it looks like someone just made a better design.
MAREN [correcting]: The instrument is one variable. The delivery system is a second. The failure mode under uterine pressure is a third. What the Somatex solved wasn't just the shunt design — it was shunt dislocation. The Harrison's straight cannula relies on friction and tissue pressure to stay in place. The uterine wall is muscular. It moves. The fetus moves. The cannula migrates. Dislocation was the dominant failure mode, and better surgical technique wasn't fixing it because it wasn't a technique problem.
CASPAR [to Maren]: It was a geometry problem.
MAREN [yes]: The parasol flanges spring open on both sides of the uterine wall after insertion. They distribute the anchoring force across a wider surface area. The shunt is held in place by geometry, not friction. That required a new delivery mechanism too — you can't deploy parasol flanges through the same cannula geometry as a straight tube. The instrument, the delivery system, and the failure mode are three linked problems that got solved together. The outcome data is the end of the story. The engineering is what happened before the story started.
JUNO [to Maren]: Can other engineers look at that outcome data and reverse-engineer what to do next?
MAREN [measured]: The outcome data tells you the geometry worked. It doesn't tell you what failure modes the next geometry will introduce. Every new anchor design creates new stress points on the uterine wall. Every new delivery mechanism creates new opportunities for misdeployment. You need bench testing, cadaveric models, iteration through failure modes that outcome studies will never capture — because outcome studies only show you what went wrong after it went wrong in a patient.
CASPAR [to Maren]: Then let's talk about the problem that outcome data has been documenting for forty years without a solution. The membrane.
MAREN [flat]: PPROM.
CASPAR [for the listener]: Preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes. When you withdraw an instrument from the uterus, you leave an access hole in the amnion — the inner membrane of the amniotic sac surrounding the fetus. The amniotic sac is the fluid-filled enclosure the fetus develops in. Its integrity is essential for lung development and for keeping the pregnancy going. The amnion does not self-seal the way skin does. In thirty to forty-six percent of fetal surgery cases, that membrane ruptures prematurely and triggers early labor.
JUNO [to Maren]: Is this a materials science problem, a geometry problem — what kind of problem is it?
MAREN [direct]: It's a biology problem that materials science keeps trying to solve and keeps failing to solve. The amnion is not a synthetic membrane. It has a cellular architecture that doesn't respond to the repair strategies we use for punctured synthetic materials. The repair window after instrument withdrawal is essentially zero at current access diameters — by the time the instrument is out, the damage is done.
JUNO [cutting in]: So sealants don't work?
MAREN [resuming]: There are groups working on membrane sealants, collagen patches, photopolymerizable hydrogels. None of them have produced a reliable clinical solution. The tissue doesn't cooperate.
JUNO [quietly]: So every instrument entering this field is implicitly trying to solve PPROM, and none of them have.
MAREN [yes]: And every instrument that makes the access point larger makes the problem worse. Instrument diameter is not an engineering preference — it's a clinical constraint with a direct line to complication rates.
CASPAR [to Maren]: Which brings us to robotics. The KU Leuven group has a flexible robotic endoscope prototype that can steer inside the uterus rather than moving in a fixed line. The problem it's designed to solve is the anterior placenta — when the placenta attaches to the front wall of the uterus, a straight fetoscope can't reach the blood vessels that need to be lasered in TTTS surgery without crossing the placenta, which risks bleeding. A steerable instrument could navigate around it.
JUNO [interested]: And the prototype works.
MAREN [careful]: In the lab. What I want to know is what happens to the actuation cables after fifty sterilization cycles.
JUNO [picking up]: That's the internal cabling that moves the tip?
MAREN [yes, clipped]: Cables, joints, any articulating component — all of it goes through the same high-heat or chemical sterilization as everything else in the surgical tray. Prototype materials are often chosen for performance, not sterilization compatibility. That's not a criticism of the engineering; it's the next engineering problem. And then there's the capital cost question. A center doing two hundred TTTS cases a year — which is high-volume — has to justify a robotic platform against an existing instrument that already works. The anterior placenta problem is real. Whether it's common enough and severe enough to drive adoption at that scale is a separate calculation.
JUNO [receiving it]: Engineering elegance is real. Clinical readiness isn't demonstrated yet.
MAREN [yes]: Those are different claims and they should stay different. The instruments I know how to build are for moving things — a shunt, a balloon, a fiber. A viral vector is not a thing you move the same way. That's a different conversation, and I'll leave it there.
JUNO [after a beat]: She handed us the hardest part on her way out.
CASPAR [quietly]: A gene therapy delivery device is not a Class II medical device like a shunt or a fetoscope. When you're delivering a viral vector — a modified virus carrying therapeutic genetic material into cells — the device is entangled with the biologic it carries. FDA review involves both the device center and the biologics center. Nobody has navigated that combined pathway for a fetal application. The regulatory category doesn't exist yet.
JUNO [working it out]: And the design problem is stranger than the regulatory one — or rather, they're the same problem wearing different faces. The shunt is just a tube. The laser fiber is just a cable. A viral vector delivery device is also, in some sense, the therapy itself. The geometry of how it enters the tissue determines where the vector goes, how much of it reaches the target, what immune response it triggers. You can't separate the instrument from what it's doing.
CASPAR [grounding]: Tippi MacKenzie — a pediatric and fetal surgeon and researcher at UCSF who has been central to in utero gene therapy research — presented a first-in-human trial design at ASGCT 2026 for treating GM1 gangliosidosis. A fatal inherited metabolic disease caused by a single gene mutation that prevents cells from breaking down certain fats and sugars, leading to progressive neurological destruction. The proposed delivery route is intravenous, into the fetal bloodstream.
JUNO [low]: The instrument doesn't exist yet.
CASPAR [building]: The instrument that would execute that delivery does not exist. The regulatory pathway for approving it does not exist. The manufacturer who would build it has not been identified. And MacKenzie's own published work flags that the geometry of delivery — the angle, depth, dispersion pattern of the injection — directly shapes both immune response and how broadly the vector distributes through fetal tissue. The design problem and the regulatory problem are tangled from the start.
JUNO [sitting with it]: The science is far enough along to design the trial. The infrastructure isn't there to run it.
CASPAR [yes]: And PPROM is still the binding constraint even here. Whatever device delivers a viral vector into the fetal bloodstream still has to enter through the uterus. The membrane rupture risk that limits every current instrument will limit this one too. That's the problem I'd most want answered — not because it's the most dramatic open question, but because it's the one that limits everything else. Instrument size. Procedure timing. How many interventions a single pregnancy can tolerate.
JUNO [closing]: MacKenzie is designing a trial for a patient she cannot ask and cannot fully see, using an instrument that hasn't been built yet, to treat a disease whose window for intervention closes before birth. You're not just solving a mechanical problem. You're making a series of guesses about a person you will never directly encounter.
CASPAR [plain]: And the Harrison shunt was thirty years of those guesses. Then someone changed the geometry, and thirty-five more babies per hundred survived. Nobody changed the biology. Nobody changed the surgeon. The object changed. Which is what makes the next object matter.
