CASPAR [direct]: After a PGT-A euploid embryo transfer, a positive cfDNA screen has a positive predictive value of 12.5 percent for aneuploidy. Confirmed aneuploidy at amniocentesis in this population: 0.1 percent. Those two numbers should change the opening sentence of that counseling conversation.
JUNO [immediate]: The third number is the one that keeps amniocentesis on the table. Overall clinically relevant CMA yield — chromosomal microarray, higher-resolution than standard karyotype — 0.5 percent. One in two hundred. Low, but those findings are actionable.
CASPAR [clarifying]: And those three figures are not interchangeable. PPV 12.5%, confirmed aneuploidy 0.1%, CMA yield 0.5% — they're answering different questions. That distinction needs to be explicit before the paper gets unpacked.
CASPAR [formal intro]: From Prenatal Diagnosis, 2026: Lu and colleagues, a large retrospective cohort of euploid embryo transfers with paired cfDNA and amniocentesis — cell-free DNA screening concordance and residual aneuploidy risk after PGT-A.
JUNO [clinical framing]: The design is what earns the evidence grade. Both cfDNA and amniocentesis results in the same cohort — that pairing is what allows a direct PPV calculation in this specific population rather than extrapolating from unselected-pregnancy data, where the pre-test probability is completely different.
CASPAR [mechanism]: Post-PGT-A patients start from a different baseline. The embryo has already been screened for aneuploidy at the blastocyst stage. Applying PPV figures from general prenatal populations to this group has been the counseling error. The denominator has been wrong.
JUNO [pressing]: So why is cfDNA being ordered at all after a euploid transfer?
CASPAR [dry]: Reflexively, mostly. Standard prenatal care pathways don't recalibrate for the pre-screened starting point. This paper is pushing back against that.
JUNO [flat]: Which means the numbers matter more, not less, because the ordering isn't stopping overnight.
CASPAR [numbers]: PPV 12.5%. In a population where the embryo was already selected for euploidy, most positive cfDNA screens are false positives. The dominant mechanism is confined placental mosaicism — the trophoblast carries a chromosomal abnormality the fetus doesn't. That's the biology that explains why the signal doesn't match the fetus.
JUNO [separating the figures]: And the 0.1% confirmed aneuploidy rate at amniocentesis is the true-positive rate after you've gone invasive. Very low. But the 0.5% CMA yield is a separate calculation — microarray picking up copy-number variants that cfDNA wasn't designed to detect at standard resolution. Different question entirely.
CASPAR [caveat]: The retrospective design has to be named before these numbers get cited. Amniocentesis uptake reflects referral patterns and patient decisions, not a systematic protocol. If patients with more alarming cfDNA results were disproportionately the ones who proceeded to amniocentesis, the confirmed aneuploidy rate could be inflated relative to a universally-offered population.
JUNO [pressing]: How much does that concern move you?
CASPAR [measured]: It's real but bounded. The direction isn't in doubt — PPV is low in a pre-screened population, and the biology of CPM predicts exactly that. You'd need the selection bias to be very large to push 12.5% into a range that changes clinical behavior. I don't think it does.
JUNO [practice impact]: So what concretely changes. The counseling sentence for a cfDNA positive after euploid transfer shifts away from a PPV figure borrowed from an unselected population toward a citable false-positive rate. And the offer of amniocentesis gets framed specifically around CMA rather than aneuploidy confirmation.
CASPAR [closing the headline]: You're not counseling toward amniocentesis to confirm whether the fetus is aneuploid. You're counseling toward amniocentesis to ask whether there's a clinically relevant copy-number variant cfDNA missed. Those are different conversations, and Lu gives you the numbers to distinguish them.

JUNO [round 1 intro]: From Prenatal Diagnosis, 2026: Shinar and colleagues, a comprehensive narrative review — fetal intracranial hemorrhage classification, etiology, and counseling framework.
CASPAR [finding]: The headline synthesis: roughly 30 percent of fetal ICH cases may have an identifiable genetic etiology, based on the review's synthesis across heterogeneous studies. The review consolidates classification schemes, a neurosonography workflow, and the neurodevelopmental outcome data parents are actually asking about.
JUNO [caveat]: Narrative review, not a prospectively enrolled cohort. The 30% figure is a synthesis across studies with variable ascertainment — not a fresh prevalence estimate.
CASPAR [clinical hook]: Still useful. Fetal ICH is a diagnosis where scattered literature has made consistent parental messaging hard. Shinar gives you a defensible order of operations for the genetic workup. Use it as a framework, not a frequency estimate.
CASPAR [round 2 intro]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Athiel and colleagues, a 10-year two-center cohort of 70 fetuses — prenatal findings and postnatal outcomes in closed spinal dysraphism.
JUNO [finding]: Limited dorsal myeloschisis dominates the lesion spectrum. Prenatal ultrasound findings are characterized in detail, and postnatal functional outcomes are reported at 18 and 36 months.
CASPAR [caveat]: Seventy fetuses over ten years across two centers. For a lesion spectrum with real heterogeneity — lipomyelomeningocele, split cord malformations, limited dorsal myeloschisis — that's a thin denominator.
JUNO [clinical hook]: For centers seeing one or two of these a year, having two-center 36-month functional data at all is a genuine contribution. Present it to families as the best characterization available, not a precise probability estimate.
JUNO [round 3 intro]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Chonnak and colleagues, a multicenter external validation study — sagittal aortic-arch assessment methods for prenatal coarctation diagnosis plus a new predictive model.
CASPAR [finding]: Ten published sagittal aortic-arch parameters are externally validated across multiple centers, and a new multivariable model outperforms any single metric in that validation dataset. Coarctation remains one of the most commonly missed lesions in prenatal cardiac screening.
JUNO [clinical hook]: A model that beats individual metrics on external validation is a meaningful step. If you're doing dedicated arch assessment, this gives you a more defensible approach than whichever single parameter you trained on.
CASPAR [caveat]: External validation of a retrospective model is not prospective screening performance. The population this gets applied to — unselected screening, variable operator experience — has a different prevalence profile than the centers that built and validated it. That prospective data doesn't exist yet, and coarctation models have had difficulty at that translation before.
CASPAR [round 4 intro]: From Fetal Diagnosis and Therapy, 2026: Taleb and colleagues, a sub-study from the RAFT trial bilateral renal agenesis arm — fetal echocardiography and hyperoxia testing after serial amnioinfusions.
JUNO [finding]: Bilateral renal agenesis — historically lethal, the RAFT trial testing serial amnioinfusions to restore amniotic fluid and support lung development. This sub-study introduces pulmonary artery pulsatility index on maternal hyperoxia testing as a functional endpoint. Decreased PA PI after amnioinfusion correlated with favorable 30-day respiratory outcomes. The mechanism: maternal oxygen drives pulmonary vasodilation in a normally developing fetal lung, so the PI response indexes pulmonary vascular reactivity.
CASPAR [evidence grade]: Sub-study. The RCT provenance of the parent trial does not transfer automatically to this endpoint. The correlation is mechanistically coherent, but the sample is small and replication hasn't happened. Worth tracking. Not yet practice-defining.
JUNO [open questions]: Two things still unsettled. Lu gives us the PPV and the CMA yield, but not a clinical profile predicting who falls in that 0.5%. Age, cfDNA result type, ultrasound findings — which post-PGT-A patients specifically warrant amniocentesis for CMA is a question this paper names but doesn't answer.
CASPAR [second open question]: And the RAFT hyperoxia endpoint needs replication in a larger sample before anyone builds a monitoring protocol around it. The correlation is there. The evidence to act on it isn't.
JUNO [close]: Lu moves the default away from reflexive amniocentesis after a cfDNA positive. It doesn't tell you when to move back toward it.
CASPAR [final]: The paper gives you the numbers to have a more honest conversation. It does not give you the algorithm for who gets amniocentesis next. All five DOIs are in the show notes.
