CASPAR [direct]: This week's lead paper changes something specific in how you counsel a patient who's already on aspirin. Not whether to start it — the question is what you tell her at 28 weeks when she asks if she's still at risk. Lin and colleagues have RCT-embedded data on that, and the short answer is: it depends where she sat on the FMF curve at 12 weeks.
JUNO [clinical]: The reframe matters. Aspirin reduces preterm PE risk — that's not in dispute. What Lin is quantifying is the residual gradient. Women who screened at the upper tail of the first-trimester FMF distribution don't drop to average risk when they start prophylaxis. They attenuate. They stay elevated relative to women who screened lower.
CASPAR [anchoring]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Lin and colleagues, a secondary analysis of a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial across Asian centers — determinants of preterm preeclampsia despite low-dose aspirin prophylaxis.
JUNO [listening]: Design first.
CASPAR [methodical]: The stepped-wedge cluster RCT earns a higher evidence grade than a retrospective cohort because all clusters eventually receive the intervention — FMF-based first-trimester screening with aspirin initiation — but the timing of crossover is randomized. That gives you within-cluster temporal comparisons and between-cluster comparisons simultaneously. Not a clean parallel-arm RCT, but substantially better than observational data for controlling the confounders that would otherwise swamp this question.
JUNO [following]: Prospective enrollment, randomized crossover structure — several of the obvious selection biases are reduced.
CASPAR [holding]: Several. The secondary-analysis ceiling is still real. This study was not powered or designed to answer what predicts breakthrough disease in treated patients. Lin is working with data that exist inside the trial structure. The finding is directionally credible. It is not confirmatory, and effect sizes from secondary analyses in this setting should carry that label when you cite them.
JUNO [clinical]: What do the numbers show?
CASPAR [delivering]: Among women who screened high-risk by the FMF combined first-trimester algorithm — maternal history, uterine artery Doppler, mean arterial pressure, PlGF, PAPP-A — and who received low-dose aspirin, residual preterm PE risk tracked with the magnitude of baseline FMF-estimated risk. Women at the upper tail of the first-trimester distribution remained at meaningfully elevated risk for breakthrough preterm preeclampsia despite prophylaxis. The gradient persists. Attenuated, but not flat.
JUNO [pressing]: The cohort caveat — I'd push back on treating that as a footnote.
CASPAR [substantive]: It isn't one. All centers are in Asia. The FMF algorithm was calibrated primarily in European populations. Baseline preeclampsia incidence, uterine artery Doppler reference distributions, PAPP-A levels — these differ across populations in ways that affect both the absolute risk estimates and the shape of the gradient. If the algorithm is miscalibrated for this cohort, the gradient Lin observes may not map onto what a European or North American center sees in its own high-risk patients.
JUNO [landing it]: Directionally credible, not directly portable for absolute numbers until there's replication in non-Asian cohorts.
CASPAR [confirming]: Right. The direction is biologically plausible and consistent with what you'd expect from a risk-attenuating intervention. The specific effect sizes should not be cited as if they were derived from your own population.
JUNO [clinical]: So what actually changes in how you counsel?
CASPAR [measured]: The aspirin initiation conversation should communicate residual risk, not zero risk. A patient who screened at 1-in-10 on the FMF algorithm is not at the same residual risk after starting aspirin as one who screened at 1-in-50. Lin gives you a framework for making that distinction explicit. What it doesn't give you is a threshold for escalating surveillance — more frequent blood pressure checks, earlier uterine artery Doppler, lower threshold for biomarker re-testing. That protocol question is raised by this paper. It's not answered by it.
JUNO [naming it]: The paper makes the case for a tiered surveillance pathway for the highest-risk aspirin-treated women. It doesn't specify what that looks like.
CASPAR [closing]: The case for building it is stronger now. The specification isn't here. Treat Lin as the rationale, not the design document.
JUNO [pivot]: Rounds — four other things this week.
CASPAR [round 1]: From BJOG, 2026: Foo and colleagues, RCOG Scientific Impact Paper number 79 — preeclampsia and maternal cardiovascular function, linking hemodynamic phenotype to treatment response and long-term risk. This is a consensus synthesis, not primary data. The core argument: preeclampsia is hemodynamically heterogeneous. High-cardiac-output, low-vascular-resistance presentations respond differently to antihypertensives than low-output, high-resistance disease where vasoconstriction dominates. The phenotype also predicts long-term cardiovascular trajectory.
JUNO [practical]: Labetalol versus nifedipine isn't a random choice if you know which phenotype you're treating.
CASPAR [holding]: The concept is sound. The operational evidence for phenotype-guided antihypertensive selection in a general MFM unit without cardiac output monitoring is not established by this paper. Foo moves the framing. It does not move the protocol for centers that don't have hemodynamic monitoring capability.
CASPAR [round 2]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Chen and colleagues, a prospective longitudinal cohort — postpartum cardiovascular and anthropometric profiles at 12 months following hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Women with HDP showed persistent differences in cardiovascular and anthropometric profiles at 12 months compared to normotensive controls. The gap documented at six to twelve weeks was still measurable at one year.
JUNO [practical]: Useful when you're making the argument for a 12-month cardiology or primary care handoff rather than discharging after the six-week check.
CASPAR [caveat]: Single-center Chinese cohort. The magnitude of the differences and the specific parameters showing persistence need replication before they anchor a formal surveillance interval. Direction is credible; the numbers aren't yet portable.
CASPAR [round 3]: From BJOG, 2026: Meler and colleagues, a retrospective cohort study — whether adding estimated fetal weight growth velocity to Doppler improves outcome prediction in term SGA fetuses. In fetuses with EFW below the tenth centile at term — the genuinely uncertain population, often with normal Doppler — adding growth velocity between serial scans improved prediction of adverse perinatal outcomes beyond Doppler alone. A fetus whose EFW is dropping across centiles carries different risk than one sitting stable at the ninth centile with identical Doppler.
JUNO [dry]: Which supports formalizing the velocity calculation rather than leaving it to gestalt. I came in thinking this was already standard practice in most units. Apparently not consistently.
CASPAR [grading]: Retrospective, single-center. Effect sizes and any threshold for action need prospective external validation. The directional finding supports what serial growth assessment is already doing in well-resourced units. This paper makes the case for making it explicit. It doesn't tell you what velocity threshold should trigger intervention.
CASPAR [round 4]: From the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Balshi and colleagues, an expert clinical review — management of multiple sclerosis during pregnancy and the reproductive years, updated for 2026. The practical output is an updated framework for which disease-modifying agents can be continued through conception, which should be discontinued and when, and which are compatible with breastfeeding. The DMT class spans injectables — interferons, glatiramer — oral agents including dimethyl fumarate and teriflunomide, and high-efficacy infusion therapies: natalizumab, ocrelizumab, alemtuzumab. Safety profiles around pregnancy differ substantially across that range, and guidance has shifted meaningfully for some of the high-efficacy agents over the past few years.
JUNO [practical]: For preconception counseling visits, this is the 2026 reference to have in hand. The neurology-obstetrics handoff — who's managing the DMT decision when she's pregnant — is exactly where MFM gets pulled in.
CASPAR [grading]: Expert review, not a systematic review with formal GRADE assessment. Evidence quality underlying individual DMT recommendations varies. Read it as a synthesis of current best practice, not a meta-analytic output. For units seeing MS patients at preconception, it's the most current consolidated reference available.
JUNO [closing]: Two things still unsettled. First: whether the Lin risk gradient replicates in non-Asian cohorts, and whether that replication is sufficient to formally define tiered surveillance thresholds for aspirin-treated women at the upper end of the FMF distribution.
CASPAR [adding]: Second: hemodynamic phenotyping in preeclampsia as an operational concept outside research centers. Foo makes the case that phenotype matters for treatment selection and long-term risk prediction. Whether cardiac output monitoring becomes standard in severe-feature preeclampsia management beyond tertiary settings — that evidence isn't in this paper.
