CASPAR [measured]: PPROM management has been a two-variable problem for a long time — gestational age and clinical signs of chorioamnionitis. Both are crude. Kacerovsky and colleagues have been doing systematic amniocentesis in PPROM patients and classifying what they find in the fluid. This week they publish the outcome data. The question the paper forces is whether the intra-amniotic category predicts neonatal risk independently of gestational age and clinical presentation.
JUNO [direct]: And the paper is careful about what it's claiming. Not an argument to tap every PPROM. It's telling you what you'd find if you did, and what that finding predicts.
CASPAR [dry]: From AJOG, 2026: Kacerovsky and colleagues, a single-center cohort with amniocentesis-based classification — intra-amniotic categories of preterm prelabor rupture of membranes and their gestational age distribution, microbial profiles, and neonatal outcomes.
JUNO [attentive]: The design choice that earns this paper its weight is pairing amniotic fluid microbial profiling with outcome tracking — not just culture positivity. Four categories: sterile intra-amniotic inflammation without detectable organisms; microbial invasion without inflammation; microbial invasion with inflammation; and neither. Those four categories are not interchangeable, and most bedside assessments can't tell them apart.
CASPAR [precise]: Sterile inflammation and microbial invasion without inflammation look identical on a fever chart. Whether the outcome data separates them is the test of whether the taxonomy earns its complexity.
JUNO [landing it]: It does. Neonatal outcomes differ across groups. Sepsis rates and composite adverse outcomes are highest in the microbial invasion with inflammation category. Sterile inflammation sits in an intermediate position — worse than neither, better than combined invasion and inflammation.
CASPAR [careful]: The gestational age distribution interacts with the category gradient in a way worth naming explicitly. Earlier gestational ages cluster with microbial invasion and inflammation. The categories carrying the worst neonatal outcomes are also the ones presenting earliest, so the risks stack. The paper doesn't fully separate the category effect from the gestational age effect. That confound is real.
JUNO [flat]: So the categories differ. Whether the microbial profile is driving the outcome or whether both are downstream of gestational age isn't cleanly resolved by this design.
CASPAR [confirming]: That's right. For counseling purposes that distinction matters — you're citing category-associated risk, not category-caused risk.
JUNO [clinical]: The microbial profiles add texture beyond the outcome gradient. Certain organisms cluster with the inflammatory categories; others appear in invasion-without-inflammation cases. That suggests different pathophysiologic pathways, which is relevant if you're thinking about antibiotic tailoring by category rather than empiric coverage.
CASPAR [grounded]: Plausible downstream application. Not supported by a trial. The jump from "categories differ" to "tailor antibiotics by category" is longer than it looks from this paper. Single-center design is the ceiling — one program's systematic amniocentesis experience. Category distribution and outcome associations need replication before that becomes a protocol.
JUNO [taking it]: For centers already performing amniocentesis in PPROM, this is a risk-stratification framework you can counsel around now. The category tells you something about neonatal risk that gestational age alone doesn't capture. For centers that don't tap — this is the paper you bring to that conversation.
CASPAR [flat]: Classification is ready to use where the infrastructure exists. Multicenter validation is what comes before it becomes a standard.
JUNO [pivoting]: Rounds — four other things this week.
CASPAR [moving]: From AJOG, 2026: Göbl and colleagues, a methodological review — how composite endpoints and fetal growth standards alter apparent treatment effects in gestational diabetes trials. Not a new trial. A lens for reading existing ones. When you swap the composite endpoint or change the fetal growth standard used to define LGA or macrosomia, the apparent magnitude of treatment benefit in GDM trials shifts substantially.
JUNO [sharp]: Two RCTs can report different effect sizes from similar interventions because of how they defined the outcome, not because the intervention performed differently.
CASPAR [confirming]: Before citing a GDM trial effect size to justify a management decision, check which growth standard and composite definition the trial used. If two papers you're comparing don't share those choices, the comparison is unreliable. The composite endpoint and the growth standard are the variable — not footnotes to the result.
JUNO [next]: From Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2026: Martins and colleagues, a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs — endometrial preparation with versus without ovulation for frozen embryo transfer. Ovulation-based protocols yielded higher live-birth rates and lower risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy compared to artificial cycles. Artificial cycles have been the scheduling-convenient default in a lot of programs. This meta-analysis puts a downstream obstetric cost on that convenience.
CASPAR [measured]: Heterogeneity in cycle protocols across included trials is substantial. Direction of effect is consistent; the magnitude is not a number you can cite as fixed. Larger prospective data with uniform protocol definitions are what actually close this. Don't attach a specific risk magnitude until they land.
JUNO [clinical]: Consistent enough to bring to a multidisciplinary conversation with REI, particularly for patients with baseline cardiovascular risk. Artificial cycles outlasted the evidence that justified them as the default — that's the version of this finding worth carrying into that conversation.
CASPAR [moving]: From AJOG MFM, 2026: Boutin and colleagues, a multicenter cohort — incremental yield of genome sequencing after exome sequencing in nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum. Genome sequencing identified additional reportable pathogenic variants in a meaningful proportion of nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum cases after nondiagnostic exome. That may change recurrence risk counseling and, in some cases, management.
JUNO [scoping]: Yield varies by phenotype and prior testing strategy. Not a blanket genome-after-exome recommendation for every hydrops presentation — incremental yield is highest where the phenotype is complex or prior testing was incomplete.
CASPAR [direct]: There was no good number to cite for the incremental yield conversation when a family asks what comes next after a negative exome. Now there is.
JUNO [last round]: From AJOG, 2026: Cenac and colleagues, a cohort study — neonatal outcomes in persistent, resolved, and absent fetal growth restriction. Neonatal outcomes differ meaningfully across the three categories. Resolved FGR does not behave like absent FGR. The trajectory carries risk information that the endpoint label discards.
CASPAR [caveat]: The abstract was not available at time of review — same situation as the Boyd preeclampsia paper from a few weeks ago. Direction is citable; specific effect sizes are not confirmed until the full publication is in hand.
JUNO [flat]: "Resolved" has been doing more reassurance work than the evidence supports. When a patient whose growth restriction has apparently resolved asks whether the risk is gone, the answer is no longer straightforwardly yes. Serial reassessment and continued surveillance are supported by the trajectory data even when the most recent EFW is above threshold.
CASPAR [closing]: Two threads to watch. Multicenter validation of the Kacerovsky intra-amniotic classification — the framework is ready to counsel around, the adoption question isn't answered. And whether the HDP signal in artificial FET cycles survives larger prospective data with uniform protocol definitions. Both are waiting on data that hasn't been collected yet.
